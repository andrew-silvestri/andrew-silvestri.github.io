What a hobby is worth

A hobby finder built the honest way round: it ranks by how good the evidence is, not by how good the claim sounds.

Ask what a hobby does for you and the internet answers with effect sizes that mostly cannot survive the obvious objection — that healthy, solvent, well-connected people take up more hobbies, and the hobby is the symptom rather than the cause. The literature on this is large, real, and almost entirely observational. So this tool does two things at once: it ranks activities by the return the published evidence would predict for a week like yours, and it states, for each one, what kind of study that number came from and what would have to be true for it to be causal.

Interactive

The finder

Describe your age, your working day, your free hours, and how much activity and company the week already contains. It returns a ranking, with an evidence bar and a headroom bar kept deliberately apart, and the confounders written under each row.

Two bars, because they are two questions

Every row carries an evidence bar and a headroom bar, and combining them into one score would destroy the only interesting information in the tool.

Evidence is how well the causal claim is established, and it has nothing to do with how large the effect is. A Mendelian randomisation study earns a long bar with a modest odds ratio; a cross-sectional survey reporting a huge association earns a short one. Headroom is how much room your stated week leaves for that particular change: somebody already meeting the physical activity guidelines has almost no headroom on exercise, and may have a great deal on company. A person and a study are different objects, and the ranking says which of the two each bar is about.

The ladder of evidence used here

TierWhat it answersWhat it still cannot answer
Randomised trialAssignment is random, so the people are comparable and the direction of the arrow is knownShort follow-up, volunteers, and the intervention is rarely the hobby as anybody actually lives it
Mendelian randomisationGenotype is fixed at conception, so the exposure cannot have been caused by the outcomeAssumes those variants act on the outcome only through the exposure — an assumption, not a finding
Longitudinal cohortExposure is measured before outcome, and confounders can be adjusted forOnly the confounders somebody thought to measure. Reverse causation is reduced, not removed

What the strongest row actually says

Physical activity is the only entry on the page with a design that addresses reverse causation directly. A two-sample Mendelian randomisation1 study across 611,583 adults used genetic variants associated with accelerometer-measured activity and found an odds ratio of 0.74 for major depression per standard deviation of activity — roughly the difference between sitting still and about fifteen minutes a day of brisk walking. The reverse path, depression causing inactivity, was not significant in the same analysis. Trials agree on magnitude: an umbrella review of 972 reviews found a median effect of −0.43 standard deviations on depression, which is in the same range as psychotherapy.

Notice what that is not. It is not a claim about a hobby. It is a claim about raising your heart rate, which many hobbies happen to do, and the tool says so rather than attributing the effect to the pastime that carried it.

The middle of the ladder

Most rows sit here: measured before the outcome, adjusted for the obvious confounders, and still not a trial. Reading books, regularly, is a good example of both the appeal and the problem. In a twelve-year cohort of older adults, book readers had a twenty per cent3 lower mortality hazard than non-readers, and the association was stronger for books than for periodicals — a dose gradient of the kind that usually strengthens a causal case. It was also adjusted for education, wealth and baseline health, and reading remains a marker for a great deal that those three variables do not capture. No one has randomised people to read for a decade, and no one is going to. The row keeps its number and keeps the caveat attached to it.

What the weakest rows say, and why they are still here

Two entries exist to keep the tool honest rather than to recommend anything. Craft and making have consistent survey associations with wellbeing and almost no clean identification, so they carry a short bar and a note that absence of evidence here is absence of good studies rather than evidence of no effect. Brain-training software carries4 the shortest bar on the page, because it has been tested properly and the result was negative: the programmes reliably improve the trained task, much less reliably improve closely related tasks, and show very little evidence of improving everyday cognition. A ranking that quietly omitted the popular thing that does not work would be less useful than one that names it.

What this is not

It is not medical advice, it is not a prediction about any individual, and it does not model your life. Every number in it is a population average from a named study, and population averages do not transfer to people without assumptions the studies cannot supply. The scoring weights — sixty per cent evidence, forty per cent headroom — are stated on the page so that you can disagree with them, which is the point of writing them down.

The rows are archetypes rather than specific pastimes, because the studies measured categories: hobby engagement5, social integration6, activity, reading. Attaching a study of social integration to one named club would be inventing precision the source does not contain.
