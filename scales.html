From bacteria to elephants, life runs on about the same power per kilogram

In 1932 Max Kleiber, an animal scientist at the University of California, found that the resting heat of warm-blooded animals1 rises with body mass to about the three-quarter power. The rule became one of biology's few laws, and it has since been stretched to cells, cities and economies, as if one law of size ran through all of them. This page tests that idea on one pair of axes: mass, and power per kilogram. It uses public data for 2,881 species, 509 stars, 382 US metropolitan areas and the energy use of nearly every country. The three-quarter law survives only inside groups of plants and animals. Between the groups, life sits on a nearly flat floor, people with fuel rise far above it, stars tilt the other way, and big cities use energy in proportion to their size.

This data does not update.

  1. Across 2,881 species, from bacteria to an elephant, the resting power of each major group averages between 0.3 and 9 watts per kilogram, where one three-quarter-power law would spread the averages about 500-fold.
  2. A resting human body gives off about 6,000 times more power per kilogram than the Sun, and heavier stars give off more per kilogram, the opposite of the tilt in animals.
  3. Fuel lifts the average person to about 28 times the power of their own body, and larger US metros produce more per person while, in published fits, using about the same household electricity per person.

Why does life stay between 0.3 and 9 watts a kilogram?

The reference unit on this page is a resting adult: about 83 watts for a 70 kg body, between the heat of a 75-watt and a 100-watt incandescent bulb, or 1.2 watts per kilogram (W/kg). In 2019 the ecologist Ian Hatton and four colleagues2 published 8,098 measurements of resting metabolism, from bacteria and single-celled algae to trees and elephants, with their code. Rerun with their method, the average for each major group of plants, animals and protists lies between 0.3 and 9 W/kg. Birds are at the top, at 8.7 W/kg; fish, amphibians and reptiles are at the bottom, at 0.29. If one three-quarter-power law joined the groups, their averages would spread about 500-fold at their typical masses, and about 30,000-fold from the smallest protist to the elephant. They spread about 30-fold.

Anastassia Makarieva and colleagues reported3 the same band in 2008 from a different compilation of 3,006 species. The tilt that Kleiber saw is real, but it lives inside each group. On Hatton's data the exponents inside the groups run from 0.66 to 0.90, and the exponent for all plants, animals and protists pooled is 0.95 (95 percent interval 0.94 to 0.95), close to one. Geoffrey West, James Brown and Brian Enquist derived three quarters in 19974 from the geometry of branching supply networks, and in 2002 West and colleagues argued5 that one line runs from mitochondria to mammals; later work found the exponent curved6 and varied from group to group. The tilt inside a group is a fitted law. The single line across groups is not supported by these data.

How firm is the band? It is well measured for what it measures, which is plants and animals at rest, and it moves when that choice moves. Sustained metabolic rates of free-ranging animals7 run up to seven times their resting rates, most often 1.5 to 5 times, and growing single cells use 10 to 20 times the power of starved ones. Temperature is the other lever. Hatton's method adjusts cold-blooded species to 20 °C; at 37 °C, the temperature of a mammal, the same groups average 1 to 16 W/kg, a range of about 15-fold. Individual species spread wider, from 0.04 to 23 W/kg for the middle 98 percent. What survives each of these choices is the comparison: life spreads tens of times where one law of size would need hundreds to tens of thousands.

Evidence: strong for the band of resting rates; its exact width depends on temperature and activity.

The Sun runs slow

The arithmetic for the Sun is short enough to show in full. The International Astronomical Union fixes8 the Sun's power output, as a nominal value, at 3.828 × 1026 watts. Its mass, from the Union's solar mass parameter divided by the gravitational constant, is 1.988 × 1030 kilograms. Divide the first by the second and the Sun gives off 1.93 × 10−4 W/kg. A resting adult gives off 1.2 W/kg, so per kilogram the body puts out about 6,000 times more power than the Sun. The Sun is bright because it is heavy, not because each kilogram works hard. Even the slowest group in Hatton's table, fish, amphibians and reptiles, does more than a thousand times better.

Stars also tilt the other way. Zeki Eker, an astronomer9 at Akdeniz University in Turkey, and colleagues compiled published masses and radii of 509 main-sequence stars, the hydrogen-burning stage that includes the Sun, in eclipsing binary pairs from 0.18 to 31 times the Sun's mass. Their luminosity, or total power, rises on one straight-line fit as about mass to the power 4, with a local slope from about 2 to 6, so power per kilogram rises as about mass to the power 3. The lightest stars in the sample give off about 0.000003 W/kg; the heaviest, at 31 solar masses, gives off 1.5 W/kg, about as much as a resting body. The reason is physical, not biological10. A heavier star has a hotter core, and its output is set by how fast radiation can leak out against its gravity; fusion keeps pace. An animal's tilt comes from how it supplies its cells.

Eric Chaisson, an astrophysicist at Harvard11, has made a long table of such numbers, which he calls free energy rate density, and argues that it rises from galaxies to stars to plants to animals to society. As a law it is weak12. A 2024 critique in the Journal of Big History argued that the measure has never been tested against other measures of complexity, so it serves as its own yardstick. Each entry also depends on where the boundary is drawn and which mass is counted. It is an ordering, and an analogy, not a fitted law.

Evidence: strong for the numbers; the opposite tilt has a known physical cause. A common law with life: none.

Fuel takes people out of the band

A body at rest uses about 83 watts. In 2025 the world used about 20,258 kilowatt-hours of primary energy per person, in Our World in Data's compilation of the Energy Institute's statistics13. Spread over the hours of a year, that is a steady 2,300 watts for every person alive, about 28 times the power of the body. The figure is about 8,600 watts in the United States, roughly 100 times the body, and about 850 watts in India, 10 times. Counted per kilogram of the people who use it, the average person with fuel runs at 33 W/kg and the average American at 120 W/kg, above the resting band of every group of living things. No other species draws power from outside its own body on anything like this scale.

Evidence: strong; national energy statistics, divided by population and by a 70 kg body.

Cities are not big organisms

The most quoted extension of Kleiber's law is to cities. In 2007 Luís Bettencourt, Geoffrey West and colleagues reported14 that a city's economic output grows faster than its population, with exponents of about 1.1 to 1.3, while its road surface and electrical cables grow slower, at about 0.8 to 0.9. The second finding invited the analogy with animals: a big city as a large organism, saving on its networks as an elephant does. The first is the opposite of an animal's tilt. The chart below repeats the economic test on current US data. Summing the Bureau of Economic Analysis's county GDP15 into 382 of the Census Bureau's 387 metropolitan areas for 2023 gives an exponent of 1.10 (95 percent interval 1.08 to 1.12). A metro ten times larger produces about 26 percent more per person.

Energy is where the organism analogy should show, and mostly it does not. In Bettencourt's table, household electricity in German cities scales with an exponent of 1.00 (interval 0.94 to 1.06), and in Chinese cities with 1.05. Michail Fragkias and colleagues found16 that fossil-fuel carbon dioxide from US metropolitan and micropolitan areas scales at about 0.93, and at about 1 once density and income are held constant. So a city ten times larger uses about ten times the household electricity and emits eight to ten times the carbon dioxide, where the tilt inside a group of species would give 5 to 8 times. Gasoline sales in US cities, at 0.79, are the exception. The networks are leaner in big cities; the people at the ends of them mostly are not.

The exponents themselves are softer than animal exponents. Elsa Arcaute and colleagues17 at University College London redrew the boundaries of cities in England and Wales thousands of ways and found that most measures scaled close to one, and that the exponents that did differ from one moved with the boundary. This page's own fit has the same weakness: one year, one definition of a metro, and GDP counted where people work. It shows output rising with size in the United States in 2023. It does not show a law of cities.

Evidence: moderate for output rising with size in US data; strong that household electricity per person does not fall with size; mixed for fuel.

Method

Life. The data are Hatton and colleagues' Metabolism.csv (8,098 rows: mass in grams, resting or basal power in watts, temperature). The method follows their own R code. The power of cold-blooded groups and bacteria is corrected to 20 °C with an Arrhenius factor (activation energy 0.6 eV); birds and mammals are not corrected. Rows without a temperature in those groups are dropped. Each species keeps its lowest power and the mass of that row, except plants, which are not aggregated. The band is the range of the geometric means of power per kilogram of the six eukaryote groups. Exponents are ordinary least-squares fits of log power on log mass; the interval for the pooled exponent is from 1,000 bootstrap samples. The 37 °C variant changes only the reference temperature. The resting human is the 70 kg row in the same table.

People with fuel. Our World in Data's total energy supply per person (kilowatt-hours a year; updated 2026-06-30), divided by 8,766 hours, gives watts per person. Per kilogram uses a 70 kg body for every country, which is an assumption.

Stars. Eker and colleagues' table on VizieR lists 586 stars; the 509 marked for their calibration are used. Luminosity is radius squared times temperature to the fourth power, in units of the IAU nominal Sun (5,772 K, 3.828 × 1026 W). Mass in kilograms uses the IAU solar mass parameter divided by the CODATA 2018 gravitational constant18.

Cities. GDP is BEA's county GDP in current dollars (CAGDP1, line 3) for 2023, summed over the counties that the Census Bureau's 2023 population file lists in each metropolitan statistical area. Where BEA reports a Virginia county together with its independent cities, the combined value is used when every part lies in the same metro. 5 metros are left out, all in Connecticut, where BEA has no 2023 values for the planning regions that the Census file uses. The fit is least squares on logarithms (R² 0.96), with a bootstrap interval. The energy exponents for cities are published fits, not this page's.

Data vintage. Hatton and colleagues, Zenodo record 3145281 (2019); Eker and colleagues (2018), VizieR J/MNRAS/479/5491; Our World in Data energy per person, years to 2025; BEA county GDP and Census metro population estimates, 2023.

Limits

The band is a statement about resting rates. Active animals, growing microbes and plants in full sun all run higher, and the bacteria in the table are mostly at rest. Mass is wet mass; in dry mass, water-rich organisms such as jellyfish would move up. National energy statistics leave out traditional biomass such as firewood, so they understate energy use in the poorest countries, and a 70 kg body for every country is a simplification. The stars are main-sequence stars between 0.18 and 31 solar masses; giants, white dwarfs and brown dwarfs are not in the sample. The city fit is one cross-section of one country, and its exponent depends on how metros are drawn.
