Skylines, played

A skyline and a spectrum analyser are the same picture. So the picture is played.

The key

How it sounds

All of this is invented — chosen, not measured. Position across the view picks a note of the city’s chord, lowest on the left. A tower’s height, as a share of this city’s tallest, to the power 1.6, sets how loud its note is, so the chord swells as the tallest towers come into view; every city’s tallest tower is equally loud. The seven loudest notes sound at once, higher ones tilted quieter; every fourth voice is a triangle wave, the rest sine; a shelf, a low-pass at 2.4 kHz and a limiter sit before the output. No two sounding notes are closer than a minor third. There is no empty horizon on a wheel, so turning changes the chord and never silences it. The line under the meter names the notes sounding now.

How it looks

The heights, the outlines and the compass order are measured; the painting is not. Each tower stands at its Wikidata height in the footprint OpenStreetMap maps for it, seen along the line from the centre of the city to it, so its width and its setbacks are its own. Where mappers have modelled a tower in 3D, every part is drawn at its own height, with its roof; where they have only mapped the outline, that outline is raised to the tower’s height.

Measured too: the light. The sun and the moon stand at their real bearings and heights for the city’s own clock, the moon in its real phase, and every face is lit by them according to the compass direction it faces, so turning toward the sun backlights the skyline and turning away lights it, as a sundial would. Drag the time to watch it move. The weather is the city’s current weather, from Open-Meteo. The sun and moon are drawn several times larger than life.

Bent a little, for the picture: in each city every tower’s width is stretched or narrowed by the same factor (between 0.65 and 1.6) so the skyline neither thins to needles nor fuses into a wall; heights are never touched. Behind the towers, a hazy low city is drawn as texture. It is invented: it is not in the data, not on the map and not in the sound.

Invented: the windows, spaced by each tower’s floor count and patterned by its recorded style or, failing that, its year; the colours of each city’s night, chosen like its key; the haze, the searchlights, the blimp and the red beacons. Depth is staged rather than optical: height falls off only gently with distance, and far towers stand a little higher and fainter, as in a painted backdrop. Windows light with the chord: the louder a tower’s note, the more of them are on. The circle at the top right is the city from above, true bearings and distances, with your view as the wedge.
