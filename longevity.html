Bats and primates outlive their size; pheasants fall well short

Bats and primates live one and a half to two and a half times as long as their size predicts; pheasants and their kin, half as long. Bigger animals live longer, by a predictable amount. Draw a line through the record lifespans of the 6,341 species whose records are sound enough to fit, against their body weight, and each tenfold gain in weight adds about 54% to the years; mammals, birds, reptiles, fish and invertebrates each get a line of their own. Against the mammal line, bats as a group live 2.5 times as long as their size predicts, across 261 species, and primates 2.1 times across 346. Tenrecs and golden moles, and opossums, reach about half of what their size predicts; against the bird line, so do the 47 pheasants, grouse, quail and chickens. The figures depend on a gap in the data: many mammals have been watched only briefly, which makes their records short and pulls the mammal line down. Corrected for that, bats come to 2.4 and primates to 2.1. Leave the briefly watched mammals out altogether and they come to 1.5 and 1.6, still above their size (the Limits section at the foot of the page shows each figure all three ways). People sit higher still: the longest verified human life, 122.5 years, is 4.1 to 5.0 times the line for a 62-kilogram mammal, depending on the same correction, though a record set among billions of people is an upper bound against species watched a few dozen times. The animals far above their line, bats, the naked mole-rat and the ocean quahog, a clam recorded at 507 years, are where biologists look for clues to slowing ageing, the case Steven Austad makes in Methuselah’s Zoo (2022).

The 7,873 species in this model run from a microscopic rotifer of 0.5 µg to a blue whale of 150 tonnes, a range in body mass of about 300 trillion to one. The longevity quotient is the lifespan of an animal divided by the lifespan that its size predicts. Above one, the animal outlives its size; below one, it falls short.

LQ = observed lifespan ÷ lifespan predicted from body mass

Mass increases from left to right, and so does the prediction, because the line only increases with size. The animals are above and below the line, and the quotient measures by how much. This figure scores each animal against the line for all animals. The rest of the page scores each animal against the line of its own class. That puts the human higher, because mammals sit lower than animals in general.

It is built by analogy with the encephalisation quotient1, which does the same thing to brain size. It is not new: Austad and Fischer introduced it for lifespan in 19912. A quotient of one means that an animal lives exactly as long as its size predicts. The interesting animals are those far from one.

Each record has a grade. Grade A is a record checked by hand against a citation, or a record that AnAge, the main ageing database, itself grades high. Grade B is a database record that the fits use. Grade C is too uncertain to use: it is shown, but kept out of all fits. The figures below use only grades A and B.

Whole groups compared

The group view combines all the species in a taxon and compares taxa. The bar is the geometric mean quotient of the members of the group. A faint line spans its lowest member to its highest. The geometric mean is the correct average for ratios. A species at four times prediction and one at a quarter of it should average to one, not to 2.1.

Of the 83 orders holding four or more grade-A or B species, the salamanders and newts of Urodela top the list at 3.01, but on a different basis. Body mass explains only 4% of the spread in amphibian lifespans. Thus amphibians are scored against the line for all animals, not against a fit of their own. The frogs and toads of Anura get 1.56 on the same basis. Among orders scored against their own class, the egg-laying Monotremata lead at 2.66, but on only four species. The large groups near the top carry more weight: the bats of Chiroptera at 2.53 across 261 species and the Primates at 2.06 across 346 species. At the bottom are the tinamous of Tinamiformes at 0.32 and the pangolins of Pholidota at 0.40. Just above them are the tenrecs and golden moles of Afrosoricida at 0.47 and the opossums of Didelphimorphia at 0.49. The ground birds of Galliformes come in at 0.52. The shrews, moles and hedgehogs of Eulipotyphla come in at 0.66. At family rank, among families with ten or more species, the evening bats of Vespertilionidae reach 3.25 over 122 species. The scorpionfishes of Scorpaenidae reach 3.16 and the gibbons of Hylobatidae 3.01. On the all-animal line, the true salamanders and newts of Salamandridae would reach 3.20 over 13 species. Colonies are not individuals

Four entries are not lifespans of an ordinary individual. They are a black coral aged at 4,265 years, a boulder star coral and a giant barrel sponge. The fourth is a glass sponge whose spicule growth rings were read as eleven thousand years. Those are ages of the colony, whose polyps live ordinary short lives. The two sponges are single animals. They are set aside with the colonies because an age read from the growth layers of a sponge is not comparable with a recorded lifespan. All four are excluded from all regressions and hidden from the rankings by default. An eleven-thousand-year colony is at the top of each sort and hides what the data actually shows.

The extremes

The highest quotients against their own group, among the grade-A and B records, belong to an ocean quahog at 47×, a black garden ant queen at 29× and a cold-seep tubeworm at 23× its predicted lifespan. Next are a freshwater pearl mussel at 16×, an olm at 15× and a rougheye rockfish at 13×. Four of those six are invertebrates. They are scored against one line pooled from 57 species of all the invertebrate phyla here, which is a coarse yardstick. The lowest belong to a silkmoth at 0.04×, a common wasp at 0.05× and a common octopus at 0.07×. Next are a longfin inshore squid at 0.07× and a toolache wallaby at 0.07×. The olm’s 100 years is a projection, not an observed age. Voituron et al. (Biology Letters 7:105, 2011) estimated a maximum of about 102 years from a laboratory population. The 68 years that the table gives as its wild maximum is the mean adult lifespan of that population. The toolache wallaby’s figure is the Amniote compilation’s 13 months for a 10 kg animal. It is very likely an error of the kind that the outlier rule removes, but the rule’s three-standard-deviation cut narrowly misses it.

Some animals outside those lists are worth a closer look. The honey bee worker and the honey bee queen have the same genome. But their lifespans are an order of magnitude apart, on different diets and in different roles in the hive. The giant Pacific octopus is large, capable, and dead in five years, because it breeds once and then stops eating. The chambered nautilus, a cephalopod that does not breed once and die, lives twenty. Labord's chameleon spends most of its existence inside an egg. It then lives four or five months as an adult, the shortest life of any four-limbed vertebrate. Its body size predicts six to eight years (eight at the 25 grams in the hand-checked table, six at the 8.73 grams from AnAge). None of this is visible in a table of lifespans sorted by years. All of it appears when body mass is divided out.

The baseline

The baseline is an ordinary least squares regression of log lifespan on log body mass, for all animals and for each group. The fit inside each group is important. Birds come out about 2.2 times longer-lived than mammals of equal mass (1.8 once the mammal baseline is corrected for study effort; see Limits). Thus a single fit across all animals gives each bird an unearned bonus and each mammal an undeserved penalty. Fish classes are pooled together. The many small invertebrate classes are pooled into one baseline, because separately they are too small to fit.

The amphibian regression returns an r² of 0.04 across 83 amphibian species. That is not a relationship. The model rejects any group fit with a non-positive slope or an r² below 0.15, and that group falls back to the global baseline. The model reports which groups it rejected and why. Amphibians are currently the only rejected group. Their orders are ranked on the global baseline and marked apart in the orders figure.

Data

The table has 7,873 species in 10 phyla, 32 classes, 199 orders, 764 families and 2,921 genera. Each record has full taxonomy, an adult body mass, at least one maximum lifespan, a quality grade and its provenance. Species are matched on the binomial alone, with no synonym resolution. Thus a species that the sources name in two ways is in the table twice. A match on common names finds 103 common names that two or more binomials carry. Among them is the African spurred tortoise, as both Centrochelys and Geochelone sulcata; some are distinct species that share a name. For 80 of those names, at least two copies are grade A or B and enter the fits. The pink cockatoo and Major Mitchell’s cockatoo, one bird under two common names, are a further pair that this check misses. 2,905 species have a maximum that their source labeled wild, 1,795 have one labeled captive, and 309 have both. The hand-checked table was built for that comparison, so the model does not let a bulk source overwrite it. The largest set, 3,482 species, has a maximum with no statement of where the animal lived. The Amniote and AmphiBIO compilations give one figure per species with no origin, and FishBase population maxima do the same. Also, 90 AnAge rows give the specimen origin as unknown. A captive maximum is longer than a wild one. Thus a record with no stated origin is carried, drawn and counted as a maximum of unrecorded origin, not as a wild one.

Disputed records lose to verified ones: the 226-year koi and the 120-year cockatoo are both out. Grade C records are in the table and in the visualiser, with their grade, but are kept out of all regressions. Thus a reader can examine a weak record, but the record cannot move the baseline that judges it.

Where the data comes from

Six databases give a maximum lifespan. They overlap a lot, and they disagree. A species in more than one database takes its numbers from the best available source, and the others are kept as corroboration. An average of a careful record and a careless one gives a number that belongs to neither.

Body-mass databases are a separate matter, and they are much larger. AVONET (birds only), PanTHERIA and Amniote together have a mass for 14,673 species. None of them creates a record: a species with a mass and no lifespan gives this model nothing to use. A record with a lifespan and no mass takes a mass from another source. This occurred 524 times: 509 from another lifespan record of the same species, and 15 from AVONET.

After the merge, 8,583 distinct species had a lifespan. 710 of them were dropped because no source gave a body mass. Most of these are reptiles and amphibians, which are usually measured snout to vent and never weighed. 641 fish stay in, with a mass calculated from maximum length through FishBase's own length–weight relationship. Those masses are modeled, not measured, and their records are marked and graded to show this. What remains is 7,873 species, graded A 149 · B 6,250 · C 1,474 as they come from their sources. The outlier rule described under the arithmetic then demotes 57 grade-B records to C. The four colonies (one grade B, three grade C) are set aside, so the model runs on A 149 · B 6,192 · C 1,528.

This is the ceiling, and the reason matters. About 2.24 million species have been described. The six databases merged here hold fewer than nine thousand with both an age and a weight. Sources that are not merged add some more (DATA_SOURCES.md, in the download, lists Add-my-Pet, SeaLifeBase and a compilation of bivalve ages among them). But a maximum lifespan needs somebody to watch an animal until it dies, and for most species nobody has. The limit is less the access to data than the absence of the observations. Wikidata is the aggregate of individual species pages, not a compiled table. It holds 1,147 taxa with a recorded maximum lifespan and 259 with a lifespan and a mass together. That is less than a twentieth of what the compiled databases give, so scraping species pages one at a time would not do better.

The arithmetic, written out

Each baseline is one ordinary least squares regression, unweighted, of log₁₀ maximum lifespan in years on log₁₀ adult body mass in grams. It uses the grade-A and grade-B records. Grade C is kept out of all fits and is scored against the result. Nothing is weighted. This rests on an assumption: an uncertain record is biased short, not merely noisy. If so, a lower weight still lets the bias through, but removal of the record does not. The simulation in test_fit_strategy.py shows this. But it marks exactly the records that it shortened, so it illustrates the choice and does not prove it. In the real table, grade C does not identify the briefly watched records (see Limits). The count beside each baseline above is the number of records in that fit. The sample spans 14.5 orders of magnitude of body mass, from 0.5 µg to 150 tonnes.

One rule runs before the final fit. A preliminary fit for each group finds each grade-B record more than 3 residual standard deviations from it, in either direction. The rule demotes these records to grade C: 57 records here (31 mammals, 10 birds, 8 fish, 8 reptiles). The Amniote compilation is grade B by construction. Among the demoted records are twenty-seven of its mammals and reptiles (twenty-five and two). They have maximum lifespans of one to three months at body masses up to five and a half kilograms. One example is a 2.2 kg hare at one month. These are unit or field errors, not observations, and they were at the bottom of each ranking on this page. The rule is symmetric, so nobody can say that it removes only the records that hurt the result. It never changes a grade-A record. Of those, 88 are rows of the hand-checked table, entered and checked by hand; the other 61 are AnAge records that AnAge itself grades high. The rule deletes nothing: a demoted record keeps its row, its note gives the reason, and the visualiser shows it under Include C.

The slope is the interesting parameter. At 0.188, it means that a tenfold increase in mass gives a 1.54-fold increase in maximum lifespan. Thus lifespan goes approximately as the 5th root of mass. For this reason, growth is a poor way to escape the prediction. An animal a thousand times heavier than another has a predicted lifespan only about 3.7 times longer.

An honourable mention

Steven Austad’s Methuselah’s Zoo: What Nature Can Teach Us About Living Longer, Healthier Lives (MIT Press, 2022) is the book behind the question this page asks. It is a working comparative biologist’s tour of the animals that outlive their size — the bats, the naked mole-rat, the quahog, the bowhead — and the argument that the interesting cases are precisely the ones a body-mass expectation fails to explain. The quotient computed here is a way of putting a number on that observation; the observation, and the case for taking it seriously, are his. It is worth reading whether or not you care about the arithmetic.

Limits

The biggest one is who has been watched. A maximum lifespan is a record, and records increase with the number of animals that somebody followed. The Amniote compilation supplies a lifespan only for species that AnAge does not have, which are the less-studied ones. It is 60% of the mammal baseline, 60% of the reptile baseline and 28% of the bird baseline. Where both databases have a species, the audit found that they agree (median difference zero). But at equal mass within a family, an Amniote-only mammal record is ×0.60 the lifespan of an AnAge record from a medium number of animals. AnAge sorts its records from tiny to huge by the number of animals behind them. The ratio is ×0.75 in reptiles and ×0.82 in birds. That decreases each baseline, so the quotient of each well-studied species above it is biased high. The figures on this page keep the pooled baseline. The site’s October 2026 quality audit measured the gap and ran two checks. Both are rebuilt in build_lq.py and run on each build. The first check rescales to the medium level each record whose source has a measurable offset, and then runs the same fits again. These sources are Amniote, and AnAge by sample size; the hand-checked rows, FishBase and AmphiBIO are not rescaled. The second check is cruder. It removes each record based on few animals or on an unknown number. This cuts the mammal line to 566 species and skews it towards the well-kept ones, so it is a bound, not a better estimate. The fish and invertebrate lines do not change in either check, because they use only hand-checked and AnAge records.

Relative to other amphibians, the lead of the salamanders is smaller than their rank suggests. Amphibians as a whole are 2.0 times above the all-animal line, so Urodela’s 3.01 is about 1.5 times the amphibian average.

With the correction, the ranking of orders almost does not change. Across the 83 orders that the two runs share, the rank correlation is 0.994, and all ten of the top ten are the same. But the correction does change the figures for single well-documented mammals, including the human. The human figure has a further problem that no correction here can fix. It is the best of billions of documented lives, against species watched a few dozen times, so it is an upper bound.

Three more limits. The model treats species as independent points, but related species share their lifespans by descent. Inside mammal families, lifespan increases more slowly with mass than it does across families. Thus “each tenfold gain in weight” is a statement across groups. The maximum mixes record types: a zoo record for one species, a banding recapture for another, a compilation figure of unknown origin for a third. Part of the position of each order comes from the record types of its members. Also, some species are in the table twice under two names (see Data), so their groups count them twice.
