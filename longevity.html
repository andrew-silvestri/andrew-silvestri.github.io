Bats and primates live one and a half to two and a half times as long as their size predicts; pheasants and their kin, half as long

Bigger animals live longer, by a predictable amount. Draw a line through the record lifespans of the 6,341 species whose records are sound enough to fit, against their body weight, and each tenfold gain in weight adds about 54% to the years; mammals, birds, reptiles, fish and invertebrates each get a line of their own. Against the mammal line, bats as a group live 2.5 times as long as their size predicts, across 261 species, and primates 2.1 times across 346, while tenrecs and golden moles, and opossums, reach about half of what their size predicts. Against the bird line, the 47 pheasants, grouse, quail and chickens reach about half too. Those figures depend on a gap in the data: many mammals have been watched only briefly, which makes their records short and pulls the mammal line down. Corrected for that, bats come to 2.4 and primates to 2.1; leave the briefly watched mammals out altogether and they come to 1.5 and 1.6, still above their size (the Limits section at the foot of the page shows each figure all three ways). People sit higher still: the longest verified human life, 122.5 years, is 4.1 to 5.0 times the line for a 62-kilogram mammal, depending on the same correction, though a record set among billions of people is an upper bound against species watched a few dozen times. The animals far above their line, bats, the naked mole-rat and the ocean quahog, a clam recorded at 507 years, are where biologists look for clues to slowing ageing, the case Steven Austad makes in Methuselah’s Zoo (2022).

The 7,873 species in this model run from a microscopic rotifer of 0.5 µg to a blue whale of 150 tonnes, a range in body mass of about 300 trillion to one. Dividing an animal’s lifespan by the one its size predicts gives the longevity quotient: above one, the animal outlives its size; below one, it falls short.

LQ = observed lifespan ÷ lifespan predicted from body mass

Mass rises left to right and so does the prediction, because the line only rises with size. The animals scatter above and below it, and the quotient measures by how much. This figure scores each animal against the line for all animals; the rest of the page scores each against its own class’s line, which puts the human higher, because mammals sit lower than animals in general.

It is built by analogy with the encephalisation quotient1, which does the same thing to brain size, and it is not new: Austad and Fischer introduced it for lifespan in 19912. A quotient of one means an animal lives exactly as long as its size predicts. The interesting animals are the ones far from one.

Each record carries a grade. A is a record checked by hand against a citation, or one AnAge, the main ageing database, itself grades high. B is a database record the fits use. C is too uncertain to use: it is shown, but kept out of every fit. The figures below use A and B only.

Comparing whole groups

The group view aggregates every species in a taxon and sets taxa against each other. The bar is the geometric mean quotient of the group's members, with a faint line spanning its lowest to its highest. The geometric mean is the correct average for ratios: a species at four times prediction and one at a quarter of it should average to one, not to 2.1.

Of the 83 orders holding four or more grade-A or B species, the salamanders and newts of Urodela top the list at 3.01, but on a different footing: body mass explains only 4% of the spread in amphibian lifespans, so amphibians are scored against the line for all animals rather than a fit of their own, the frogs and toads of Anura coming out at 1.56 the same way. Among orders scored against their own class, the egg-laying Monotremata lead at 2.66, but on only four species. The large groups near the top carry more weight: the bats of Chiroptera at 2.53 across 261 species and the Primates at 2.06 across 346 species. At the bottom sit the tinamous of Tinamiformes at 0.32, the pangolins of Pholidota at 0.40, the tenrecs and golden moles of Afrosoricida at 0.47, the opossums of Didelphimorphia at 0.49; the ground birds of Galliformes come in at 0.52 and the shrews, moles and hedgehogs of Eulipotyphla at 0.66. At family rank, among families with ten or more species, the evening bats of Vespertilionidae reach 3.25 over 122 species, the scorpionfishes of Scorpaenidae 3.16, the gibbons of Hylobatidae 3.01 (on the all-animal line, the true salamanders and newts of Salamandridae would reach 3.20 over 13 species). The scorpionfish figure is mostly rockfishes: 43 of its 47 species are Sebastes, a genus the sources file partly under Sebastidae and partly under Scorpaenidae, and without them the family comes to 1.98.

Colonies are not individuals

Four entries are not lifespans of an ordinary individual: a black coral aged at 4,265 years, a boulder star coral, a giant barrel sponge, and a glass sponge whose spicule growth rings have been read as eleven thousand years. Those are ages of the colony, whose polyps live ordinary short lives; the two sponges are single animals, set aside with them because an age read from a sponge’s growth layers is not comparable with a recorded lifespan. They are excluded from every regression, and hidden from the rankings by default, because an eleven-thousand-year colony sits at the top of every sort and buries whatever the data actually shows.

What comes out

The highest quotients against their own group, among the grade-A and B records, belong to an ocean quahog at 47×, a black garden ant queen at 29×, a cold-seep tubeworm at 23×, a freshwater pearl mussel at 16×, an olm at 15× and a rougheye rockfish at 13× its predicted lifespan. Four of those six are invertebrates, scored against one line pooled from 57 species of every invertebrate phylum here, a coarse yardstick. The lowest belong to a silkmoth at 0.04×, a common wasp at 0.05×, a common octopus at 0.07×, a longfin inshore squid at 0.07× and a toolache wallaby at 0.07×. The olm’s 100 years is a projection, not an observed age: Voituron et al. (Biology Letters 7:105, 2011) estimated a maximum of about 102 years from a laboratory population, and the 68 years the table carries as its wild maximum is that population’s mean adult lifespan. The toolache wallaby’s figure is the Amniote compilation’s 13 months for a 10 kg animal, very likely an error of the kind the outlier rule removes, which the rule’s three-standard-deviation cut misses by a hair.

Some animals outside those lists are worth a closer look. The honey bee worker and the honey bee queen carry the same genome and sit an order of magnitude apart in lifespan, on different diets and in different roles in the hive. The giant Pacific octopus is large, capable, and dead in five years, because it breeds once and then stops eating. The chambered nautilus, a cephalopod that does not breed once and die, lives twenty. Labord's chameleon spends most of its existence inside an egg and then lives four or five months as an adult, the shortest life of any four-limbed vertebrate, in a body whose size predicts six to eight years (eight at the 25 grams the hand-checked table records, six at the 8.73 grams AnAge gives). None of this is visible in a table of lifespans sorted by years. All of it appears the moment body mass is divided out.

Fitting the baseline

An ordinary least squares regression of log lifespan on log body mass, run globally and within each group. Fitting inside a group matters. Birds come out about 2.2 times longer-lived than mammals of equal mass (1.8 once the mammal baseline is corrected for study effort; see Limits), so a single fit across every animal charges every bird a bonus it did not earn and every mammal a penalty it does not deserve. Fish classes are pooled together, and the many small invertebrate classes are pooled into one baseline, because separately they are too thin to fit.

The amphibian regression returns an r² of 0.04 across 83 amphibian species, which is not a relationship. Any group fit with a non-positive slope or an r² below 0.15 is rejected, and that group falls back to the global baseline. The model reports which groups were rejected and why. Amphibians are currently the only one, and their orders are ranked on that baseline, marked apart in the orders figure.

Data

7,873 species spanning 10 phyla, 32 classes, 199 orders, 764 families and 2,921 genera. Every record carries full taxonomy, an adult body mass, at least one maximum lifespan, a quality grade and its provenance. Species are matched on the binomial alone, with no synonym resolution, so a species the sources name two ways is in the table twice: matching common names finds 103 common names carried by two or more binomials, the African spurred tortoise as both Centrochelys and Geochelone sulcata among them (some are distinct species that share a name), and for 80 of those names at least two copies are grade A or B and enter the fits. The pink cockatoo and Major Mitchell’s cockatoo, one bird under two common names, are a further pair that check misses. 2,905 have a maximum their source labeled wild, 1,795 one it labeled captive, and 309 have both — the comparison the hand-checked table was built for, which is why no bulk source is allowed to overwrite it. The largest set, 3,482 species, carries a maximum whose source did not say where the animal lived: the Amniote and AmphiBIO compilations report one figure per species with no origin, FishBase population maxima do the same, and 90 AnAge rows list the specimen origin as unknown. A captive maximum runs longer than a wild one, so a record whose origin is not stated is carried, drawn and counted as a maximum of unrecorded origin rather than as a wild one.

Disputed records lose to verified ones: the 226-year koi and the 120-year cockatoo are both out. Grade C records appear in the table and in the visualiser, with their grade shown, but are held out of every regression, so a weak record can be looked at without being allowed to move the baseline that judges it.

Where the data comes from

Six databases carry a maximum lifespan. They overlap heavily and they disagree, so a species found in more than one takes its numbers from the best source available and records the others as corroboration. Averaging a careful record against a careless one produces a number that belongs to neither.

Body-mass databases are a separate matter and they are much larger. AVONET, which covers birds only, PanTHERIA and Amniote between them hold a mass for 14,673 species. None of them creates a record: a species with a mass and no lifespan is not an observation of anything this model can use. A record that has a lifespan and no mass takes one from elsewhere, which happened 524 times: 509 from another lifespan record of the same species, and 15 from AVONET.

8,583 distinct species carried a lifespan after the merge. 710 of them were dropped for having no body mass in any source — mostly reptiles and amphibians, which are routinely measured snout to vent and never put on a scale. 641 fish were kept with a mass computed from maximum length through FishBase's own length–weight relationship; those are modeled, not measured, and are marked and graded accordingly. What survives is 7,873 species, graded A 149 · B 6,250 · C 1,474 as they arrive from their sources. The outlier rule described under the arithmetic then demotes 57 grade-B records to C, and the four colonies (one grade B, three grade C) are set aside, so the model runs on A 149 · B 6,192 · C 1,528.

This is the ceiling, and it is worth being clear about why. About 2.24 million species have been described. The six databases merged here hold fewer than nine thousand that have been both aged and weighed. Sources not merged add some more (DATA_SOURCES.md, in the download, lists Add-my-Pet, SeaLifeBase and a compilation of bivalve ages among them), but a maximum lifespan requires somebody to have watched an animal until it died, and for most species nobody has. The limit is less access to data than that the observations were never made. Wikidata, which is the aggregate of individual species pages rather than a compiled table, holds 1,147 taxa with a recorded maximum lifespan and 259 with a lifespan and a mass together — less than a twentieth of what the compiled databases give, which is the answer to whether scraping species pages one at a time would do better.

The arithmetic, written out

One ordinary least squares regression per baseline, unweighted, of log₁₀ maximum lifespan in years on log₁₀ adult body mass in grams, over the grade-A and grade-B records. Grade C is held out of every fit and scored against the result. Nothing is weighted. That rests on an assumption: that an uncertain record is biased short, not merely noisy, in which case down-weighting still lets the bias through and dropping does not. The simulation in test_fit_strategy.py shows that, but it marks exactly the records it shortened, so it illustrates the choice rather than proving it; in the real table grade C does not single out the briefly watched records (see Limits). The count beside each baseline above is the number of records in that fit. The sample spans 14.5 orders of magnitude of body mass, from 0.5 µg to 150 tonnes.

All animals:    log10 L = +0.583 +0.188 log10 M
Mammals:        log10 L = +0.357 +0.215 log10 M
Birds:          log10 L = +0.706 +0.216 log10 M
Reptiles:       log10 L = +0.605 +0.211 log10 M
Fish:           log10 L = +0.379 +0.240 log10 M
Invertebrates:  log10 L = +0.593 +0.256 log10 M

predicted lifespan  =  10 ^ (a + b * log10(mass in grams))
LQ                  =  observed maximum lifespan / predicted

One rule runs before the final fit. A preliminary fit per group flags any grade-B record more than 3 residual standard deviations from it, in either direction, and demotes it to grade C: 57 records here (31 mammals, 10 birds, 8 fish, 8 reptiles). The Amniote compilation grades as B by construction, and among the records the rule demotes are twenty-seven of its mammals and reptiles (twenty-five and two) with maximum lifespans of one to three months at body masses up to five and a half kilograms — a 2.2 kg hare at one month — which are unit or field errors, not observations, and which sat at the bottom of every ranking on this page. The rule is symmetric so that it cannot be accused of only removing what hurts, it never touches a grade-A record (88 of those are rows of the hand-checked table, entered and checked by hand; the other 61 are AnAge records that AnAge itself grades high), and it deletes nothing: a demoted record keeps its row, its note says why, and the visualiser shows it under Include C.

The slope is the interesting parameter. At 0.188 it says a tenfold increase in mass buys a 1.54-fold increase in maximum lifespan — lifespan goes roughly as the 5th root of mass. This is why the prediction is so hard to escape by growing: an animal a thousand times heavier than another is predicted to live only about 3.7 times longer.

An honourable mention

Steven Austad’s Methuselah’s Zoo: What Nature Can Teach Us About Living Longer, Healthier Lives (MIT Press, 2022) is the book behind the question this page asks. It is a working comparative biologist’s tour of the animals that outlive their size — the bats, the naked mole-rat, the quahog, the bowhead — and the argument that the interesting cases are precisely the ones a body-mass expectation fails to explain. The quotient computed here is a way of putting a number on that observation; the observation, and the case for taking it seriously, are his. It is worth reading whether or not you care about the arithmetic.

Limits

The biggest one is who has been watched. A maximum lifespan is a record, and records grow with the number of animals anyone followed. The Amniote compilation supplies a lifespan only for species AnAge lacks, which are the less-studied ones, and it is 60% of the mammal baseline, 60% of the reptile one and 28% of the bird one. Where both databases carry a species the audit found they agree (median difference zero), but at equal mass within a family an Amniote-only record sits at ×0.60 the lifespan of an AnAge record resting on a medium number of animals (AnAge sorts its records from tiny to huge by how many animals stand behind them) in mammals, ×0.75 in reptiles and ×0.82 in birds. That pulls each baseline down, so every well-studied species above it reads high. The figures on this page keep the pooled baseline. The site’s October 2026 quality audit measured the gap and ran two checks, both rebuilt in build_lq.py and run on every build. The first rescales every record whose source’s offset can be measured (Amniote, and AnAge by sample size; not the hand-checked rows, FishBase or AmphiBIO) to the medium level and re-runs the same fits. The second, cruder, leaves out every record resting on few or an unknown number of animals, which cuts the mammal line to 566 species and skews it towards the well-kept ones, so it is a bound rather than a better estimate. The fish and invertebrate lines do not move in either: they are built from hand-checked and AnAge records only.

Relative to other amphibians, the salamanders’ standing is smaller than their rank suggests: amphibians as a whole sit 2.0 times above the all-animal line, so Urodela’s 3.01 is about 1.5 times the amphibian average.

Under the correction the ranking of orders barely moves: across the 83 orders the two runs share, the rank correlation is 0.994, and all ten of the top ten are the same. The figures for single well-documented mammals, the human’s included, are where the correction bites. The human figure has a further problem no correction here reaches: it is the best of billions of documented lives, set against species watched a few dozen times, so it is an upper bound.

Three more. Species are treated as independent points, but related species share their lifespans by descent, and inside mammal families lifespan rises more slowly with mass than it does across them, so “each tenfold gain in weight” is a statement across groups. The maximum mixes record types: a zoo record for one species, a banding recapture for another, a compilation figure of unknown origin for a third, and part of any order’s position is which kind its members have. And some species are in the table twice under two names (see Data), which counts them twice in their groups.
