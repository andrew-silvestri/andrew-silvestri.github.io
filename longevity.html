Bats and primates outlive their size; pheasants fall well short

Bats and primates live one and a half to two and a half times as long as their size predicts; pheasants and their kin, half as long. Draw a line through the record lifespans of the 6,301 species whose records are sound enough to fit, plotted against body weight, and each tenfold gain in weight adds about 55% to the years; mammals, birds, reptiles, fish and invertebrates each get a line of their own. Against the mammal line, bats as a group live 2.5 times as long as their size predicts, across 260 species, and primates 2.1 times across 339. Tenrecs and golden moles, and opossums, reach about half of what their size predicts, and against the bird line so do the 47 pheasants, grouse, quail and chickens. How firm are those figures? They lean on a gap in the data: many mammals have been watched only briefly, which makes their records short and pulls the mammal line down. Corrected for that, bats come to 2.4 and primates to 2.1; leave the briefly watched mammals out altogether and they come to 1.5 and 1.6, still above their size (the Limits section at the foot of the page shows each figure all three ways). People sit higher still. The longest verified human life, 122.5 years, is 4.1 to 5.0 times the line for a 62-kilogram mammal, depending on the same correction, though a record set among billions of people is an upper bound against species watched a few dozen times. The animals far above their line — bats, the naked mole-rat and the ocean quahog, a clam recorded at 507 years — are where biologists look for clues to slowing ageing, the case Steven Austad makes in Methuselah’s Zoo (2022).

Bigger animals live longer, and that is the least interesting thing about them. The 7,791 species in this model run from a microscopic rotifer of 0.5 µg to a blue whale of 150 tonnes, a range in body mass of about 300 trillion to one, and across that range the heavier animal usually does outlive the lighter one, by an amount regular enough to draw as a line. The animals worth studying are the ones that miss it. The longevity quotient measures the miss: it is the lifespan of an animal divided by the lifespan that its size predicts. Above one, the animal outlives its size; below one, it falls short. But a quotient is only as sound as the line it divides by, and the line depends on a choice: which records count. Much of what follows is about that choice.

LQ = observed lifespan ÷ lifespan predicted from body mass

Mass increases from left to right, and so does the prediction, because the line only rises with size; the animals sit above and below it, and the quotient measures by how much. This figure scores each animal against the line for all animals, while the rest of the page scores each against the line of its own class. That puts the human higher, because mammals sit lower than animals in general.

It is built by analogy with the encephalisation quotient1, which does the same thing to brain size, and it is not new: Austad and Fischer introduced it for lifespan in 19912. A quotient of one means that an animal lives exactly as long as its size predicts. The interesting animals are those far from one.

How sound is any one record? Each carries a grade. Grade A is a record checked by hand against a citation, or a record that AnAge, the main ageing database, itself grades high. Grade B is a database record that the fits use. Grade C is too uncertain to use, so it is shown but kept out of all fits. The figures below use only grades A and B.

Which groups outlive their size?

The group view combines all the species in a taxon and compares taxa. The bar is the geometric mean quotient of the members of the group, and a faint line spans its lowest member to its highest. Why the geometric mean? It is the correct average for ratios: a species at four times prediction and one at a quarter of it should average to one, not to 2.1.

Of the 83 orders holding four or more grade-A or B species, the salamanders and newts of Urodela top the list at 2.89, but on a different basis: body mass explains only 9% of the spread in amphibian lifespans, so amphibians are scored against the line for all animals, not against a fit of their own. The frogs and toads of Anura get 1.62 on the same basis. Among orders scored against their own class, the egg-laying Monotremata lead at 2.66, but on only four species. The large groups near the top carry more weight: the bats of Chiroptera at 2.54 across 260 species and the Primates at 2.05 across 339 species. At the bottom are the tinamous of Tinamiformes at 0.32 and the pangolins of Pholidota at 0.40, and just above them sit the tenrecs and golden moles of Afrosoricida at 0.47 and the opossums of Didelphimorphia at 0.49. The ground birds of Galliformes come in at 0.52. The shrews, moles and hedgehogs of Eulipotyphla come in at 0.66. At family rank, among families with ten or more species, the rockfishes of Sebastidae reach 3.46 over 46 species. The evening bats of Vespertilionidae reach 3.29 and the capuchins and squirrel monkeys of Cebidae 3.25.

Colonies are not individuals

Four entries are not lifespans of an ordinary individual: a black coral aged at 4,265 years, a boulder star coral, a giant barrel sponge, and a glass sponge whose spicule growth rings were read as eleven thousand years. The coral ages are ages of the colony, whose polyps live ordinary short lives. The two sponges are single animals, but they are set aside with the colonies, because an age read from the growth layers of a sponge is not comparable with a recorded lifespan. All four are excluded from all regressions and hidden from the rankings by default. Shown, an eleven-thousand-year entry goes to the top of every sort and hides what the data actually shows.

Furthest from the line

The highest quotients against their own group, among the grade-A and B records, belong to an ocean quahog at 47×, a black garden ant queen at 29× and a cold-seep tubeworm at 23× its predicted lifespan. Next are a freshwater pearl mussel at 16×, a rougheye rockfish at 13× and a Brandt's bat at 12×. Four of those six are invertebrates, scored against one line pooled from 57 species of all the invertebrate phyla here, which is a coarse yardstick. The lowest belong to a silkmoth at 0.04×, a common wasp at 0.05× and a common octopus at 0.07×. Next are a longfin inshore squid at 0.07× and a mountain sundamys at 0.07×.

Some animals outside those lists are worth a closer look. The honey bee worker and the honey bee queen have the same genome, yet their lifespans are an order of magnitude apart, on different diets and in different roles in the hive. The giant Pacific octopus is large, capable, and dead in five years, because it breeds once and then stops eating. The chambered nautilus, a cephalopod that does not breed once and die, lives twenty. Labord's chameleon spends most of its existence inside an egg and then lives four or five months as an adult, the shortest life of any four-limbed vertebrate, though its body size, 8.73 grams in both AnAge and Amniote, predicts about six years. None of this is visible in a table of lifespans sorted by years. All of it appears when body mass is divided out.

A line for each group

The baseline is an ordinary least squares regression of log lifespan on log body mass, fitted for all animals and again for each group. Fish classes are pooled together, and the many small invertebrate classes are pooled into one baseline, because separately they are too small to fit. Why fit inside each group at all? Birds come out about 2.2 times longer-lived than mammals of equal mass (1.8 once the mammal baseline is corrected for study effort; see Limits). A single fit across all animals would give each bird an unearned bonus and each mammal an undeserved penalty.

Amphibians are currently the only rejected group, so their orders are ranked on the global baseline and marked apart in the orders figure.

Data

The table has 7,791 species in 10 phyla, 32 classes, 199 orders, 751 families and 2,896 genera, and each record has full taxonomy, an adult body mass, at least one maximum lifespan, a quality grade and its provenance. A species that the sources name in two ways, such as the African spurred tortoise, as both Centrochelys and Geochelone sulcata, is merged into one record before anything is fitted. Two names are merged when they carry the same common name and their species epithets agree, or when the crosswalk between bird taxonomies in AVONET pairs them one to one, which is how the pink cockatoo and Major Mitchell’s cockatoo become one bird. Seven more names are merged by a hand list, each pair only where a source here states the synonymy in its own words: FishBase’s remarks file the largetooth sawfish’s two other names under Pristis pristis, for example. The merged record takes its numbers from the best source, like any other, and keeps the other name in its note. The merge folded 115 second names into 113 species. After the merge, 33 common names are still carried by two or more binomials. Some are species that one source splits in two and another does not, some are distinct species that share a name, and a few may be one species under two names that no rule joins and no source here states, such as the sperm whale as both Physeter catodon and P. macrocephalus; for 26 of those names, at least two copies are grade A or B and enter the fits. Where the sources file one genus under two families, as they do for 50 genera here, every species of the genus takes the family that its highest-ranked source gives, so the groupers of Epinephelus are scored with Serranidae, as AnAge files them, not split between it and Epinephelidae.

Then there is the question of where the animal lived. 2,889 species have a maximum that their source labeled wild, 1,795 have one labeled captive, and 308 have both. The hand-checked table was built for that comparison, so the model does not let a bulk source overwrite it. The largest set, 3,415 species, has a maximum with no statement of where the animal lived: the Amniote and AmphiBIO compilations give one figure per species with no origin, FishBase population maxima do the same, and 91 AnAge rows give the specimen origin as unknown. A captive maximum is longer than a wild one, so a record with no stated origin is carried, drawn and counted as a maximum of unrecorded origin, not as a wild one.

Disputed records lose to verified ones: the 226-year koi and the 120-year cockatoo are both out. Grade C records are in the table and in the visualiser, with their grade, but are kept out of all regressions. A reader can examine a weak record, but the record cannot move the baseline that judges it.

Where the data comes from

Six databases give a maximum lifespan, and they overlap a lot and disagree. A species in more than one of them takes its numbers from the best available source, with the others kept as corroboration, rather than from an average. An average of a careful record and a careless one gives a number that belongs to neither.

Body-mass databases are a separate matter, and they are much larger: AVONET (birds only), PanTHERIA and Amniote together have a mass for 14,673 species. None of them creates a record, since a species with a mass and no lifespan gives this model nothing to use. A record with a lifespan and no mass takes a mass from another source, which happened 557 times: 542 from another lifespan record of the same species, and 15 from AVONET.

After the merge, 8,464 distinct species had a lifespan, and 673 of them were dropped because no source gave a body mass. Most of these are reptiles and amphibians, which are usually measured snout to vent and never weighed. The exception is 620 fish, kept with a mass calculated from maximum length through FishBase's own length–weight relationship. Those masses are modeled, not measured, and their records are marked and graded to show this. What remains is 7,791 species, graded A 145 · B 6,217 · C 1,429 as they come from their sources. The outlier rule described under the arithmetic then demotes 58 grade-B records to C, two more are demoted by name for the reason given there, and the four colonies (one grade B, three grade C) are set aside, so the model runs on A 145 · B 6,156 · C 1,486.

This is the ceiling, and the reason matters. About 2.24 million species have been described, and the six databases merged here hold fewer than nine thousand with both an age and a weight, under half of one percent of them. Sources that are not merged add some more (DATA_SOURCES.md, in the download, lists Add-my-Pet, SeaLifeBase and a compilation of bivalve ages among them). But a maximum lifespan needs somebody to watch an animal until it dies, and for most species nobody has. The limit is less the access to data than the absence of the observations. Wikidata, the aggregate of individual species pages rather than a compiled table, holds 1,147 taxa with a recorded maximum lifespan and 259 with a lifespan and a mass together. That is less than a twentieth of what the compiled databases give, so scraping species pages one at a time would not do better.

The arithmetic, written out

Each baseline is one ordinary least squares regression, unweighted, of log₁₀ maximum lifespan in years on log₁₀ adult body mass in grams, over the grade-A and grade-B records. Grade C is kept out of all fits and is scored against the result. Why leave the uncertain records out rather than weight them down? The choice rests on an assumption: an uncertain record is biased short, not merely noisy. If so, a lower weight still lets the bias through, and removing the record does not. The simulation in test_fit_strategy.py shows this, but it marks exactly the records that it shortened, so it illustrates the choice rather than proving it; in the real table, grade C does not identify the briefly watched records (see Limits). The count beside each baseline above is the number of records in that fit. The sample spans 14.5 orders of magnitude of body mass, from 0.5 µg to 150 tonnes.

One rule runs before the final fit. A preliminary fit for each group finds each grade-B record more than 3 residual standard deviations from it, in either direction, and the rule demotes these records to grade C: 58 records here (31 mammals, 10 birds, 8 fish, 8 reptiles, 1 amphibian). The Amniote compilation is grade B by construction, and among the demoted records are twenty-seven of its mammals and reptiles (twenty-five and two), with maximum lifespans of one to three months at body masses up to five and a half kilograms. One is a 2.2 kg hare at one month. These are unit or field errors, not observations, and they were at the bottom of every ranking on this page. Two more records are demoted by name before the preliminary fit, with the reason in each note: the Amniote compilation’s 13 months for the 10 kg toolache wallaby and its 10 months for the 2.3 kg lesser cane rat, very likely errors of the same kind, for which no other source here records a lifespan.

The rule is symmetric, so nobody can say that it removes only the records that hurt the result. It never changes a grade-A record: of those, 87 are rows of the hand-checked table, entered and checked by hand, and the other 58 are AnAge records that AnAge itself grades high. Nor does the rule delete anything. A demoted record keeps its row, its note gives the reason, and the visualiser shows it under Include C.

The slope is the interesting parameter. At 0.189, it means that a tenfold increase in mass gives a 1.55-fold increase in maximum lifespan, so lifespan goes approximately as the 5th root of mass. Growing is a poor way to escape the prediction. An animal a thousand times heavier than another has a predicted lifespan only about 3.7 times longer.

An honourable mention

Steven Austad’s Methuselah’s Zoo: What Nature Can Teach Us About Living Longer, Healthier Lives (MIT Press, 2022) is the book behind the question this page asks. It is a working comparative biologist’s tour of the animals that outlive their size — the bats, the naked mole-rat, the quahog, the bowhead — and the argument that the interesting cases are precisely the ones a body-mass expectation fails to explain. The quotient computed here is a way of putting a number on that observation; the observation, and the case for taking it seriously, are his. It is worth reading whether or not you care about the arithmetic.

Limits

The biggest limit is who has been watched. A maximum lifespan is a record, and records rise with the number of animals that somebody followed. The Amniote compilation supplies a lifespan only for species that AnAge does not have, which are the less-studied ones, and it makes up 60% of the mammal baseline, 59% of the reptile baseline and 27% of the bird baseline. Where both databases have a species, the audit found that they agree (median difference zero). But at equal mass within a family, an Amniote-only mammal record is ×0.60 the lifespan of an AnAge record from a medium number of animals, on AnAge’s own scale, which sorts its records from tiny to huge by the number of animals behind them. The ratio is ×0.75 in reptiles and ×0.81 in birds. That lowers each baseline, so the quotient of every well-studied species above it is biased high.

The figures on this page keep the pooled baseline. The site’s October 2026 quality audit measured the gap and ran two checks, both rebuilt in build_lq.py and run on each build. The first rescales to the medium level each record whose source has a measurable offset, and then runs the same fits again; those sources are Amniote, and AnAge by sample size, while the hand-checked rows, FishBase and AmphiBIO are not rescaled. The second check is cruder. It removes each record based on few animals or on an unknown number, which cuts the mammal line to 562 species and skews it towards the well-kept ones, so it is a bound, not a better estimate. Neither check moves the fish or invertebrate lines, because they use only hand-checked and AnAge records.

The salamanders’ lead over other amphibians is smaller than their rank suggests. Amphibians as a whole sit 2.1 times above the all-animal line, so Urodela’s 2.89 is only about 1.4 times the amphibian average.

With the correction, the ranking of orders barely changes: across the 83 orders that the two runs share, the rank correlation is 0.994, and all ten of the top ten are the same. But the correction does change the figures for single well-documented mammals, including the human. The human figure has a further problem that no correction here can fix. It is the best of billions of documented lives, set against species watched a few dozen times, so it is an upper bound.

Three more limits. The model treats species as independent points, but related species share their lifespans by descent, and inside mammal families lifespan rises more slowly with mass than it does across families. So “each tenfold gain in weight” is a statement across groups. The maximum also mixes record types (a zoo record for one species, a banding recapture for another, a compilation figure of unknown origin for a third), and part of the position of each order comes from the record types of its members. And the merge of synonyms (see Data) joins only the names its two rules and its hand list find, so a species renamed in a way that none of them sees is still in the table twice and counted twice in its groups.
