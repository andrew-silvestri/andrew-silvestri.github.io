A kilogram of Brazilian beef carries the emissions of a 230-kilometer drive

Food miles are mostly a distraction. Beef from cleared Brazilian pasture puts as much greenhouse gas into the air as a petrol car driven that far, and most of it is the animal: methane from the cow's gut (55%) and the forest cleared for its pasture (25%), against 0.2% for shipping it to Texas. The other high-footprint foods here follow the same pattern (cheese, chicken, heated-greenhouse tomatoes, rice and coffee). At least 56% of each one's emissions happen before it leaves the country or state it was grown in, while shipping by sea or rail is at most a quarter of any food's footprint on its usual route (25% at the highest). Flying is what changes the picture: a banana flown instead of shipped by sea comes out nearly ten times the shipped one (9.8 times). Yet even the beef's figure rests partly on an accounting rule. The model charges 28% of the animal's emissions to its hide, tallow and other co-products; with the whole animal charged to the meat, the beef would be about 310 km of driving, and it is the largest footprint here either way.

This data does not update.

  1. A kilogram of beef is 4.4 times a kilogram of hard cheese and 13 times a kilogram of chicken, because a cow's gut makes methane and this herd's pasture was cut from forest.
  2. Heating a greenhouse adds about 2.4 kg to every kilogram of tomatoes on every sea route the model runs, more than twice the whole footprint of a field tomato on the same route.
  3. The tanning is most of the footprint of a pair of leather shoes: 67% of it. The shoe's share of the cow, the part that footprints argue over, is 2% of the pair.

A kilogram of beef from a Brazilian herd, eaten in Texas, comes to 53.1 kg of CO2e, a unit that counts the warming effect of every gas as carbon dioxide. A petrol car with one person in it emits 0.24 kg a kilometer, counting its share of the factory and the road. So the kilogram of beef is 230 km of driving.

Split by source, 80% is two things that happen on the ranch: methane from the cow's gut and the forest cleared for its pasture. Everything after the animal leaves the farm comes to 1.5%, and the 9,598 km ocean crossing, the leg that food-miles arguments are about, is 0.2% of the total. At under 1%, the voyage barely registers.

The same model covers twenty items in three groups (food, transport and clothing), 28 producing regions and four freight modes: sea, road, rail and air. Select an item, where it was made and where it ends up, and the calculator traces the chain stage by stage. It works out every answer afresh rather than looking up a stored one, so any combination works, including the absurd ones.

What the model finds

The table gives each item on one typical route, in kilograms of CO2e, a unit that counts every gas as the amount of carbon dioxide that would warm the planet as much over a century1. For food the route runs from where it was grown to where it is eaten; for a vehicle or a garment, from where it was made to where it is used. None of these figures is a measurement. Each is an estimate built from representative inputs, and several of the largest turn on a choice the total does not show: how to split a shared burden between co-products, or how long a car or an aircraft lasts. The sections below show where that choice sits and how far it moves the answer.

Putting a flight, a pair of jeans and a tomato in one model lets each of them answer the same question, and some of the answers translate neatly. A cotton t-shirt, worn and washed weekly for three years, costs about as much as 30 kilometers of driving alone; a pair of trainers costs about as much as 190 grams of beef. Why does a long-haul flight come out below a short one per kilometer? Take-off and climb burn the most fuel, and a long flight spreads them over more distance: fuel alone is 12.5 kg per 100 passenger-km short haul and 9.2 long haul.

The growing, not the journey

The two tomatoes make the point most clearly. The model runs 756 sea routes between two different regions, and on each, heat for a Dutch-style glasshouse adds between 2.40 and 2.45 kg to a kilogram of tomatoes, more than 2 kg on every route, while the journey stays the same for both. On one shared route, the Netherlands to New York, the heated tomato is 5.2 times the field one. Food miles are what people reach for, and by sea they are usually the wrong lever. A banana crosses 7,821 km by sea and still comes in at 0.90 kg, because sea freight costs almost nothing per tonne-kilometer2 (one tonne carried one kilometer). Put a field tomato on a plane instead and it comes out 18 times higher. Distance matters a great deal by air, and over long hauls by truck, and much less by sea or rail.

The field tomato is the exception to the pattern above. Its footprint is small, and most of it comes after the farm: growing it is 10% of the total, and the packing, the shipping, the shop and the tomatoes thrown away are most of the rest. Beef is the opposite. Its methane and land clearing happen before the animal leaves the ranch, so the route is small beside them, and even air freight, the one shipping choice that shows, adds only about 16%.

How far down does a footprint go?

The model takes three inputs: an item, where it was produced, and where it ends up. It returns the chain as a line of stages from the field to the bin, and under each stage a tree of branches that shows where that stage's emissions came from. The branches have branches. A tomato needs fertiliser3, the fertiliser needs ammonia, the ammonia needs natural gas, and the extraction of that gas leaks methane.

The figure shows that chain, traced by the same engine the calculator runs, with the calculator's display cutoff switched off; at its default, the calculator drops the gas step as too small to list. Each box is what the tomato is charged for that process. Notice how fast the charge falls: two orders of magnitude in three steps, from the field to the gas well.

Allocation, where the argument is

Every stage carries a multiplier: the share of that stage that belongs to the product. The multiplier is there because few stages make only one thing. A slaughterhouse also makes leather and tallow, a dairy cow also becomes beef, and a coffee mill also sells pulp. These other outputs are co-products, and splitting a stage's burden between them is called allocation. Charging the whole burden to the one output of interest is a choice too, and in many published footprints it is a choice nobody mentions. Here it sits on every stage, next to the basis it rests on. A share set where the products separate also applies to everything that went into that stage, so the slaughterhouse's 72% to the meat covers the whole animal: its gut methane, feed, manure and cleared pasture.

The cheese is that argument in its purest form. How much of a dairy cow's methane belongs to her milk rather than her meat is a judgement, and for cheese the judgement is most of the answer, because the herd stages are 93% of the kilogram. The figure below runs every published method, taken from Flysjö4 and colleagues' comparison of the methods. System expansion, which credits the cow's meat by counting the beef it replaces elsewhere, puts the same kilogram of French cheese at 8.9 to 10.6 kg. The physical split that Flysjö's team calculated for Swedish milk gives 11.7, and the International Dairy Federation5's 2015 formula for a typical herd (88% to milk, the model's default) gives 12.1. Economic value gives 12.6, protein 12.8 and mass 13.4. So what is the footprint of a kilogram of cheese? That depends on the rule: the highest over the lowest, taking the bottom of the system-expansion range, is ×1.50. If the milk carried the whole cow, it would be 13.7, but that is the case with no split between milk and meat, not a basis, so the figure shows it for scale and the spread leaves it out.

The leather shoe is the same argument where it barely applies. A hide is a co-product of a beef animal, and the model gives it 2.2% of the animal. That share is an assumption, below the 2.7% economic mean Lunesu6 and colleagues measured, and it comes to 2% of the pair (under 2% before rounding). Moving to the highest physical share they found takes the pair from 10.6 to 11.0 kg, ×1.03. One caveat applies even to that small share: the figure for the animal behind it has no source. Most of the shoe's footprint is the tanning, which serves only the leather and is charged in full. The hide is where the argument is; the tanning is where the carbon is. Allocation decides the answer where the shared stage is the answer, and nowhere else.

Lifetime, allocation by another name

A car’s factory does not belong to any one journey, and neither does an airframe. A per-kilometer figure charges you one part in a lifetime — the vehicle divided by the distance it will travel before it is scrapped — and that lifetime is an assumption, not a measurement. The model assumes 200,000 km for a car, and the calculator lets you change it. Retire the petrol car at half that distance and it comes to 26.1 kg per 100 km instead of 23.6; keep it twice as long and it is 22.3. The electric car carries more of its burden in the factory and less in fuel, so it moves further: 18.4 at half the life, 12.8 at the default, 10.0 at double. For a flight, the same control is the airframe’s life in passenger-kilometers, so the model charges you your share of building the aircraft, divided in the same way as your share of the fuel. Until someone decides how long a vehicle lasts, it has no footprint per kilometer.

How would a wrong total be caught?

Every total must fall inside a band drawn from published figures, and the run fails if one falls outside. For food, most bands come from Poore and Nemecek's survey of the published literature7, and a band is a range rather than a single figure because these are distributions across thousands of farms. The field tomato's band runs a little below their range, and the model's 0.67 kg sits in that lower part. Most vehicle and clothing bands carry the figure they were drawn from: the UK government's conversion factors, Levi Strauss's assessment of its own jeans, and an MIT study of a running shoe. The bands are wide. A result inside one is a weaker test than a match with the study, which the jeans and the trainers do not achieve.

The test checks more than totals. The parts of every stage must sum to its total, the cheese's milk share must reach the processes beneath it, and a change of route or grid must move the answer the way physics says it should. Last, it checks that the corrected inputs below are the values their sources give.

One test exists because of a bug that hid well. The model charged production-side electricity at the grid of the consuming region, so a French dairy looked dirty whenever someone in India ate the cheese. No single run shows the bug; it appears only when the destination changes and something moves that has no business moving. A test now walks a fixed list of food production stages and fails if any of them draws power from the wrong grid. The clothing and vehicle stages, and coffee roasting, are not on that list.

Corrections, 24 October 2026

A review of this site's pages in October 2026 found four inputs that did not agree with their own sources or notes, and correcting them moved the totals below. The leather shoe moved most. Its tanning sat inside the hide's stage, so the hide's 2.2% share of the animal applied to the tanning as well; charged in full, the pair is 10.56 kg, not 3.66. Second, in October 2025 the IPCC corrected a calculation error in the table of warming potentials that this page cites. Methane from cattle's guts, rice paddies, coffee lagoons and landfill now counts 25.3 times carbon dioxide (it was 27). Nitrous oxide counts 267 (273), and the methane that leaks from gas wells counts 28.1 (the model had used an older report's 28).

Third, the soil factor now counts the nitrous oxide that leaves later from nitrogen that washes or blows off the field, as the IPCC chapter it cites does. The factor is 5.76 kg CO2e per kilogram of nitrogen, against the 4.4 the model used before for the direct part alone. Fourth, the cheese's milk share is now the International Dairy Federation's formula that the page cites (88%), in place of a Swedish figure from another study (85%).

Limits

The largest open question is the size of the co-product shares. Each share applies to the whole of what went into its stage, so the beef's total moves almost in proportion to its 72%: the stages that share reaches are 99% of the kilogram. Apart from the cheese's milk share, none of the food shares has a named source; each is an assumption.

The model may also count food losses twice. It increases every stage before the bin to include the food lost along the way, but the comment beside the stage amounts says that the amounts already include losses. Which is correct? Without the derivation of the amounts, the model cannot say. If the losses are already included, the food totals would decrease by 6% to 34%, most for the field tomato and the banana.

Several inputs are representative values, not measurements of any farm or factory. The beef's land-use figure is a Brazilian pasture-expansion figure, and it does not change when the calculator is set to another origin. Some terms mix methane and nitrous oxide without a record of the split (manure, the chicken sheds and the hide's animal), so they stay at the old warming table, which overstates them by up to about 7%. The soil factor applies IPCC's wet-climate leaching to every field, and the disposal of every garment is priced as food waste in landfill, at most 4% of the total of any garment. Freight factors do not include the production and delivery of the fuel, which would increase them. Almonds carry a contested credit for carbon held in orchard trees. The grid figures have the label operating margin (the power plants that respond to extra demand) but look like annual averages. Which is correct matters most for the electric car and the train, and the model cannot say by how much.

An explainer, not a footprint for any real product or a basis for buying or reporting.
