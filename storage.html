Big batteries in Texas earn most of their money in rare years, when a handful of days of extreme prices pay for half the year, and 2025 was not one of them

A grid battery makes money by charging when power is cheap and selling it back when it is dear. This page runs one with four hours of storage on ERCOT's real prices (ERCOT's grid serves about 90 per cent of Texas's electricity demand) for every year from 2011 to 2025, and lets it know the coming month's prices in advance. That gives a ceiling on what it could earn trading energy. The ceiling was $208 a year for each kilowatt of the battery's power in 2023, and $66 in the median year. The big years were the ones with a few days of extreme prices: half of 2021's money came in 4 days of February, the month of Winter Storm Uri. 2025 earned the median $66, but no day stood out: its best earned $2.33 per kilowatt, and it took 72 days, more than any year since 2011, to earn half its money. Read from the market monitor's chart, real batteries earned about $14 per kilowatt from energy in 2025.

What a battery is paid for

A battery on the grid fills up when electricity is cheap, often in the middle of a sunny or windy day, and empties when it is dear, usually in the evening as the sun sets. Its profit on a day is what it sold the power for, less what it paid, less a little for the wear of cycling. This page puts a battery of one megawatt with four hours of storage on ERCOT's real-time prices, every hour since 2011, at the average of ERCOT's four trading hubs, and lets it see the coming month's prices before it acts. No real battery knows the future, so every dollar figure here is a ceiling for trading energy at the hub. For scale, owning a battery like this costs about $242 per kilowatt a year (NREL's Annual Technology Baseline1), more than the ceiling in any year since 2011; real batteries also earn from being paid to stand ready as reserves, which this page does not model.

What real batteries earned

ERCOT's independent market monitor2 reports what the whole battery fleet earned, calling batteries ESRs, energy storage resources. Its chart for 2021 to 2025 has no table; read by eye, it puts the fleet's income from selling energy at about $14 per kilowatt in 2025. This page's ceiling for a two-hour battery that year, about the fleet's average size (1.9 hours), is $45, so real batteries took in roughly a third of what perfect foresight at the hub allows. Some of the gap is that they cannot see the future; some, that they sit at their own points on the grid and joined the fleet through the year; some, that they hold charge back to be paid as reserves. The monitor on 2025: "Annual revenue per unit of ESR capacity declined significantly in 2025, falling by almost 37% from 2024 after normalization for the larger volume of ESR capacity in the market."

Batteries also move the prices they trade on. In the monitor's words: "The increase in solar capacity primarily drove the reduction in daytime price spikes from 2024 to 2025, while growth in energy storage capacity largely drove the decline in price spikes during solar ramp periods." That is the market monitor's finding; this page does not test it.

Method

Prices. ERCOT's historical settlement point prices3 for its hubs and load zones, real-time (15-minute, averaged to the hour) and day-ahead (hourly), one file a year from 2011, the first full year of its current market, to 17 October 2026. The battery trades at HB_HUBAVG, the average of the four trading hubs. The daylight-saving days have 23 and 25 hours, as ERCOT settled them; one 15-minute price ERCOT's 2013 file leaves out (3 November 2013, the first 02:00 hour) is filled from the other three in that hour. Checked against an independent record of the same market: ERCOT's day-ahead North hub price in 2015 agrees with the on-peak trades EIA publishes4 for that hub to 2.4 per cent over the year.

The battery. An optimisation, a linear program5 with one yes-or-no choice per hour, that maximises profit over 30 days at a time with every price in that window known in advance: one megawatt, four hours of storage, 86 per cent round-trip efficiency6 (the share of the energy put in that comes back out), a cycling cost of $1 per megawatt hour charged and the same discharged, and no charging and discharging in the same hour. Each year starts half full, and each 30-day window carries its charge into the next, so the battery places no value on charge left at a window's end. Checked against a price pattern whose best answer is known in closed form; it is reproduced exactly.

Days. A day's profit is the cash of its hours: sales less purchases less the cycling cost. "Days for half" sorts the days that made money from best to worst and counts how many it takes to reach half of their total; "share from best 10 days" is the same total's share in its ten best days. On day-ahead prices the ceilings run $27 to $219 per kilowatt, and the five best years are the same five, though 2022's took 38 days to reach half.

Duration. The same years with one, two and eight hours of storage, real-time, at the hub average. Each extra hour of storage adds less than the one before:

Limits

Perfect foresight is a ceiling, not a forecast: a real operator bids without knowing tomorrow's prices. The model trades energy only, at hourly average prices, so it misses the reserve markets, where batteries earned most of their money until 2024 (the monitor: "Prior to 2024, ESRs participated in the wholesale market primarily through ancillary services because they can respond quickly to fluctuations in supply and demand."), and the extra spread inside each hour. Its four hours of storage give about 3.71 usable hours at full power, because its losses come out of the stored energy. The ownership cost above is NREL's capital and fixed operations cost for a four-hour battery, with the capital spread over twenty years at an assumed eight per cent; NREL gives these batteries a 15-year life, and over 15 years the same arithmetic gives about $271. Either is a cost reference, not a verdict, since a real battery has other income. This is an explainer, not investment advice.

The code

Python: the fetch script, which records where and when each ERCOT file came from; the price reader; the dispatch model; the payload builder; the page generator; and the tests. The archive carries the payload, not ERCOT's price files, which the fetch script downloads again. Also the Julia and Octave ports of the dispatch and a workbook of the same arithmetic. 345 KB.
