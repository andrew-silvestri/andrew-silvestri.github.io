Every living cell holds back a voltage, and life is the work of keeping it

A living cell and a dead one are made of much the same molecules. One of the first things to differ is a voltage. Living cells keep their inside from about ten to a couple of hundred millivolts away from their outside, across a skin about 5 nanometres thick, and pay without pause to keep it there. One leading hypothesis says the first cells borrowed such a difference from warm vents in the sea floor. A human brain, 2 percent of the body, takes a fifth of its oxygen, much of it to pump ions back after its nerves fire; and on one influential view, pain and pleasure report how far the body has drifted from where it must stay. Even the question of what life is for comes back to that held-back difference.

This data does not update.

  1. A mitochondrion holds 150 to 200 millivolts across a membrane about 5 nanometres thick, a field of 30 to 40 million volts a metre, about ten times what makes dry air spark; other cells hold from about ten to two hundred.
  2. A leading hypothesis says life began on a ready-made gradient: modern Lost City vent fluid against a modelled early ocean is worth up to about 207 millivolts, the same order as a mitochondrion's 172, though no experiment has shown a vent making life.
  3. In 85,991 comfort votes, 60 percent wanted no change at 23 to 24 °C and requests to change rose on both sides, the U-shape a set point predicts, though thermal preference is not pain.

Where did the first voltage come from?

Nobody knows how life began, and no experiment has yet gone from simple chemistry to a cell. Several research programmes offer answers. One puts the start in warm ponds on land that dried and refilled1; another in chemistry driven by sunlight on cyanide and sulphur compounds near the surface2. A third, argued since the late 1980s by the geochemist Michael Russell and later by the evolutionary biologist William Martin and the biochemist Nick Lane, puts it in alkaline hydrothermal vents on the sea floor3. It is the one that starts with a voltage.

The living example is Lost City, a field of pale carbonate chimneys on the flank of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, found in 20004. Seawater reacts with mantle rock below the field and comes back warm, rich in hydrogen and strongly alkaline. The MARHYS database collects the published measurements5. Across 70 Lost City fluid records with a pH value (61 samples and 9 calculated end-members, from eight studies), the median pH is 10.1, and the median temperature of 131 records is 64 °C. The early ocean was more acidic than today's: a 2018 reconstruction puts its pH6 at about 6.6 some four billion years ago (95 percent range 6.3 to 7.2).

How much push is that? A difference in acidity is a difference in the concentration of protons, and it can be stated as a voltage. Each pH unit is a tenfold step in concentration, worth 59.2 millivolts at 25 °C and 68.1 millivolts at 70 °C (the Nernst factor7, RT ln10 / F). The vent median less the ocean value is 3.5 pH units, and 3.5 × 59.2 gives about 207 millivolts, or 238 at 70 °C. That is an upper bound for an ideal barrier. Even so, it is the same order as the push in a working mitochondrion, which holds about 1728.

But a gradient in rock is not a gradient across a cell. The thin mineral walls proposed as the first membranes are modelled as micrometres thick, not nanometres, and a 2016 critique argued11 that a vent's natural gradient was unlikely to be put to work before protein machinery existed. Laboratory tests are few: in a 2020 experiment12 a pH gradient across a thin iron-nickel sulphide wall turned carbon dioxide into formate, a one-carbon molecule, at about 1.5 micromoles a litre, but only with fluid at pH 12.3 against 3.9. At pH 9.1, close to Lost City, only traces too small to measure formed, and no carbon-carbon bonds formed in any run. The pond camp raises its own objections: vents dilute what they make, and salty water works against the formation of membranes.

Evidence: hypothesis. The arithmetic is physics. No experiment has shown that the first cells used a vent's gradient, and other origins remain open.

Cells harvest a gradient; nerves spend one

In 1961 Peter Mitchell13, a British biochemist then at the University of Edinburgh, proposed that cells make ATP, their energy currency, by pumping protons across a membrane and letting them flow back through an enzyme. Many biochemists resisted the idea for years; he received the 1978 Nobel Prize in Chemistry alone14. The enzyme, ATP synthase, is a rotor. In cattle mitochondria a ring of eight subunits turns once for every eight protons and makes three ATP, so each ATP costs about 2.7 protons15. The push behind those protons is 150 to 200 millivolts across about 5 nanometres16, a field of 30 to 40 million volts a metre. Dry air breaks down into a spark17 at about 3.

Animals run the same machinery in the other direction. A neuron spends ATP on a pump that moves three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in18. Potassium then leaks back out through open channels, and the charge it carries holds the inside some 50 to 70 millivolts below the outside. When a channel opens, sodium falls back in, the voltage flips, and the flip runs along the fibre as a spike. In the squid nerve that Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley measured in 193919, the swing was 80 to 95 millivolts. Keeping that charge ready is expensive. The brain is about 2 percent of body mass yet takes about 20 percent of the body's oxygen at rest20. A 2012 model of rat cortex21 put most of its signalling cost into pumping sodium back out after synapses and spikes.

A nervous system is not needed for the trick. When a Paramecium, a single-celled swimmer, bumps into something, the touch opens channels at its front end, calcium flows in, a spike reverses the beat of its cilia22 and the cell backs away. Nervous systems came late. The oldest widely accepted traces of life23 are about 3,480 million years old; the oldest fossil brain24, in a 520-million-year-old arthropod from south-west China, is some 2,960 million years younger. For most of its history, life steered by membrane voltages alone.

Evidence: strong. Chemiosmosis, the sodium pump and the spike have been measured for decades. The brain's energy split comes from a model of rat tissue. The timeline rests mostly on fossils, which give only minimum ages, and on one model estimate.

Is pain a gradient?

Not literally. Pain is an experience, not a voltage. The International Association for the Study of Pain defines it as an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience26 linked to actual or potential tissue damage, or resembling one that is, and keeps it separate from nociception, the nerve signal that may or may not produce it. But physiology has long described the body as holding many quantities near set points: Claude Bernard wrote of a stable internal environment in 1865, and Walter Cannon named the process homeostasis in 192627. Blood pH, for one, stays between 7.35 and 7.4528.

In 1971 Michel Cabanac showed29 that the same warm stimulus feels pleasant to a chilled person and unpleasant to an overheated one. In 2003 Bud Craig argued that pain is a homeostatic emotion30, a feeling that reports the body's state and drives a fix. On that reading, pleasure and pain are a felt slope that steepens with distance from the set point. The slope is a metaphor; the set points are measured.

The starkest evidence that pain does a job comes from people born without it. In 2006 a team traced31 one form of congenital insensitivity to pain to the gene SCN9A in three families in northern Pakistan. The boy who led doctors to them performed street stunts that would have hurt anyone else, and he died after jumping from a house roof on his 14th birthday. A rarer form also stops sweating, and that kills through heat as much as through the missing pain: a 1994 review of about 31 published cases32 found that almost a fifth of the children died of high fever within their first three years.

Can the felt slope be measured? Thermal comfort is a field where large public data record how people feel about distance from a set point. The ASHRAE Global Thermal Comfort Database II holds33 109,033 questionnaires from field studies, mostly in offices. Of these, 85,991, from 665 buildings, record both the indoor air temperature and whether the person wanted to be warmer, cooler or left as they were.

The pattern is the one a set point predicts. Between 23 and 24 °C, 60 percent wanted no change. The share asking for change rose on both sides: half wanted to be warmer between 18 and 19 °C, and two thirds wanted to be cooler between 30 and 31 °C. The point where requests for warmer and cooler balance is 22.7 °C (95 percent interval 22.4 to 23.0). Even there, four people in ten wanted something else.

What people ask for barely moves with climate, even though what they call neutral does. In this analysis the temperature people call neutral rises by about 0.28 °C per degree of mean outdoor temperature, close to the 0.31 that the adaptive model in the ASHRAE 55 standard34 fits to naturally ventilated buildings. The temperature they ask for moves by 0.04 °C per degree on one method and 0.01 on another (interval −0.03 to 0.07), not clearly different from zero. Michael Humphreys and Fergus Nicol reported35 the same split in 2004, in field studies drawn from two large databases.

Evidence: moderate. The votes are measured. Reading them as a homeostatic signal is an interpretation, and thermal preference is not pain. The database is a compilation of separate studies, not a random sample of people.

What is the end goal?

This section is about interpretation, not measurement.

Biology gives a narrow answer, and the narrow answer is worth having. Organisms plainly pursue goals: a Paramecium backs away, a hand pulls back from a hot pan, a body sweats until its core cools. In 1958 the biologist Colin Pittendrigh proposed the word teleonomy36 for this, to describe end-directed behaviour without implying a purpose set by a mind. In 1974 Ernst Mayr sharpened the distinction37: teleonomic behaviour is aimed by a program the organism inherits. Natural selection wrote the programs, and selection has no foresight. It keeps what happened to persist and copy.

Physics adds a constraint, not a purpose. Erwin Schrödinger wrote in 1944 that life avoids decay to equilibrium by drawing order from its surroundings, and in a note added to later printings38 said that for physicists he would have put it as free energy. Some researchers go further. Jeremy England argues39 that driven matter tends to settle into forms that absorb and dissipate energy well, and Karl Friston's free energy principle holds40 that anything that persists must act as if it minimises surprise. England's case rests on idealised models, and Friston and colleagues write that the principle, like Hamilton's principle of least action, cannot be falsified as mathematics41. Neither shows that life aims at anything. Nor does life dominate Earth's energy flow: plants and algae capture about a thousandth of the sunlight Earth absorbs42 as new growth.

What is left is a description. Every cell alive today descends from a line that has not once run down to equilibrium since the last universal common ancestor, about 4.2 billion years ago on a 2024 estimate43. Each ancestor in that line copied itself before it stopped. Starve a cell of energy and the difference goes quickly: when a mould's respiration was blocked in 196544, its membrane voltage fell from about 200 millivolts to about 30 within a minute. Science finds no goal behind the line, only the work of keeping the difference long enough to copy it. Whether that work has a meaning is a question science cannot settle.

Method

Vent gradient. The Lost City values are the raw-fluid and end-member rows of the MARHYS Database 3.0 (Diehl and Bach) whose site names contain Lost City: 155 rows. Of these, 70 have a pH and 131 have a temperature. The database does not record the temperature at which the pH was measured, so the page gives the voltage at 25 and at 70 °C. The voltage is the Nernst equivalent of a pH difference: 1000 × RT ln10 / F millivolts per pH unit, with the 2018 CODATA constants. This value is an upper bound for an ideal barrier. It is not a measured potential. The ladder chart shows a range of 170 to 257 millivolts, from the 95 percent range of the ocean pH and the two temperatures.

Comfort votes. The analysis puts indoor air temperature into bins of 1 °C and uses bins with at least 200 votes. The chart shows the unbroken run of these bins, 15 to 37 °C. The balance point is the temperature at which the share that wants warmer equals the share that wants cooler, found by linear interpolation between bins; a logistic fit gives a second estimate. The neutral temperature is the temperature at which the mean thermal sensation vote crosses zero. Each slope is a regression of one of these temperatures, estimated within bands of prevailing mean outdoor temperature, on the mean outdoor temperature of each band. A band needs at least 500 votes, and an estimate outside the band's own data is dropped. Intervals are 95 percent bootstrap intervals from 1,000 resamples of whole buildings, so that votes from one building do not count as independent votes. For the slopes, each band is resampled on its own.

Voltages. The ladder shows magnitudes only. Every value except the vent comes from a measurement in a named paper or BioNumbers entry. The bars show the reported range, or the mean with its reported spread. The field strength is 150 to 200 millivolts divided by 5 nanometres.

Limits

Lost City has vented for more than 100,000 years45, on modern rock. It is an analogue for a Hadean vent, not a sample of one. The pH of the early ocean is a model estimate. The comfort database is a compilation of field studies that researchers contributed, and it is weighted towards some climates and building types. Its preference question offers only three answers. The neutral slope depends on the range of outdoor temperatures used: with the coldest and sparsest bands included it is flatter. Thermal preference is not pain; the page uses it as a large public record of how people judge distance from a set point. The brain's energy split comes from a model of rat tissue. Fossil dates are minimum ages, and nervous systems fossilise rarely. The end-goal section reports what researchers argue. It settles nothing.
