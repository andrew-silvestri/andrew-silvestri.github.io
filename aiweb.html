People still search, but fewer searches end on a website

On 19 February 2024 Alan Antin, an analyst at Gartner1, a research firm, predicted that the volume of searches on traditional search engines would fall 25 percent by 2026, as people put their questions to chatbots instead. In October 2026 Sundar Pichai, Alphabet's chief executive, told investors2 that Google's “year-over-year query growth rate is the highest it’s been in the last five years”, and search revenue had grown 12 percent in a year (to $63.35 billion a quarter). The forecast missed. This page sets five futures commonly predicted for the web against what has been measured so far. The change Gartner was reaching for is still happening, in a different place: fewer searches end with a visit to a website.

This data does not update.

  1. The best-known forecast missed: Google's search volume did not fall 25 percent by 2026, and its search revenue grew 12 percent in a year.
  2. What is shrinking is the share of searches that end on a website: people clicked a result on 8 percent of searches with an AI summary against 15 percent without one.
  3. Bots make about 53 percent of web traffic, most of it old-fashioned automation; AI writes about half of new English articles but only 14 percent of what Google shows.

How is the map drawn?

Most predictions about the web are claims about two shares: how much of its traffic is software rather than people, and how much of what readers see was written by machines. The map puts the first across and the second up. Four futures sit on it. In the first, the web adapts to AI as it adapted to search engines and phones. In a hollowed web, AI answers stand in for the visit. In an agentic web, software agents browse and buy for their owners. In a synthetic flood, machines write most of what is published and most of what is shown. The fifth future, a closing web, is about access rather than authorship, so it is drawn as a strip below the map. Where each future sits is this page's judgment, placed against today, since none of them has machine traffic or machine writing falling. The markers for today are measurements.

Today sits just past the middle across and low on the map, though how low depends on the measure. Automated software made 53 percent of the traffic that Imperva, a security firm, saw3 on its customers' sites in 2025; Cloudflare, a network company4, counted a similar 53 percent of requests for web pages as non-human on 2 December 2025, by its own definition (most of either is old-fashioned automation, not AI). AI wrote 49.9 percent of new English-language articles in early 2026, in a study by one company using three commercial detectors, but only 14 percent of the pages Google showed in mid-2025. The arithmetic is short: 49.9 divided by 14 is 3.56, so the AI share of new articles is three and a half times its share of Google's results. Part of that gap is age rather than filtering, since Google's results include pages written before chatbots existed, and the two samples are three quarters apart. The map's vertical axis uses what Google shows, because that is what readers see.

Do searches still end on websites?

The hollowed web is the future news publishers have put a number on: their executives expect search traffic to fall 43 percent within three years. Its mechanism is simple. An AI answer at the top of the results page settles the question, so the reader never visits the pages it was drawn from. Search engines once crawled pages in exchange for sending readers to them. In July 2025 Cloudflare counted5 about 5.4 crawls by Google for every visit Google sent back; for OpenAI the figure was 1,091, and for Anthropic 38,066. Whatever the old ratio was, a crawler that takes a thousand pages for each reader it returns is not keeping the bargain search engines struck.

The click data point the same way, as a rate. The Pew Research Center tracked6 the browsing of American adults in March 2025 (900 people, 68,879 searches). They clicked a result on 8 percent of searches that showed an AI summary and on 15 percent of those that did not, and clicked a link inside the summary on 1 percent. SparkToro, which studies search behavior7, estimates that 68.01 percent of American Google searches in early 2026 ended with no click at all, up from 60.45 percent in 2024; that is the high end of the published range. Fewer clicks per search can still mean as many visits, if there are more searches. For news, they do not: Chartbeat, which measures traffic8 for news sites (more than 2,500 of them), found Google search traffic to them down 33 percent worldwide in the year to November 2025.

Outside news, the counts disagree. Google's head of search, Liz Reid, wrote in August 2025 that total organic click volume “has been relatively stable year-over-year”9, without publishing the data. Graphite, a search-marketing agency10, estimated that organic Google traffic to American sites (more than 40,000 of them) fell only 2.5 percent in 2025, with the ten largest up 1.6 percent on average. In Graphite's data the losses gather where an answer can replace the visit: news, health and recipes. In Chartbeat's count11 they also gather on small publishers, which lost 60 percent of their search traffic against 22 percent for large ones. Wikipedia, which relies on visits12 for its donors and volunteers, counted about 8 percent fewer human page views in recent months of 2025 than in the same months of 2024, after it reclassified some traffic as bots. The visit is disappearing first from the sites whose pages make the best answers.

Evidence: strong that fewer searches end in a visit, and that news sites get fewer visits; moderate on how much of the drop AI answers cause. Most of the data come from browsing panels and from firms that sell traffic services, and other changes to search happened at the same time.

Are machines becoming the web's main readers?

The agentic web goes one step further: software agents, not people, do the browsing. In 2025 Microsoft announced what it called the open agentic web13, Google published a protocol for agents to make payments14, and OpenAI published one for agents to buy15 inside ChatGPT. The Model Context Protocol16, Anthropic's standard for connecting AI models to outside tools, had more than 10,000 public servers by December 2025. McKinsey, a consultancy17, projects that agents could arrange $3 trillion to $5 trillion of commerce by 2030, a forecast with nothing yet measured against it.

Software already makes most of the web's requests. Automated software made 53 percent of the traffic Imperva saw in 2025, up from 51 percent a year before. Most of it is old-fashioned automation: scrapers, scanners, attack tools and search crawlers. Declared AI crawlers made 4.2 percent of requests for web pages on Cloudflare's network in 2025, about as many as Google's own crawler at 4.5 percent. Agents working for a person are a small slice of that traffic, and no two vendors yet count them the same way. In October 2026 HUMAN Security, which tracks them18, found that 85.2 percent of agent requests went to product and search pages and 1.9 percent reached a checkout or payment page, a share of requests rather than of tasks.

Buying is the weak link. OpenAI launched Instant Checkout, purchases inside ChatGPT, in September 2025, and removed it in March 2026, sending checkout to merchants19' own apps and sites in a change it called temporary. In a 2025 Forrester survey20, 24 percent of American online adults said they would trust an agent to make routine purchases for them. Agents browse. People still pay.

Evidence: strong that software makes most requests; weak that agents act for people at scale. Every traffic share comes from a company that sells bot management, and each defines a bot its own way.

Is the web closing its doors?

The closing web is a reaction to the other two futures. A site that loses visitors to AI answers has less reason to let AI companies read it. In 2024 Shayne Longpre and colleagues21 at the Data Provenance Initiative audited the websites behind three large AI training sets. In a single year, instructions to crawlers in robots.txt files had come to restrict 5 to 7 percent of the text, up from about 1 percent; for the most important sources the restricted share was 20 to 33 percent.

News publishers moved fastest. By late 2025 about 79 percent of the top news sites in the United States and Britain blocked at least one AI training crawler, according to BuzzStream22, a marketing firm. Across the million most-visited websites23, about 10.6 percent fully blocked OpenAI's crawler in September 2025, a narrower measure that explains part of the gap. Closing came with a price list. By one count24, 91 licensing deals between publishers and AI companies were announced or reported between January 2023 and June 2026. News Corp's deal with OpenAI25 is reported at about $250 million over five years; Amazon's with The New York Times26 at $20 million to $25 million a year, about 1 percent of the paper's revenue.

The gates leak. TollBit, which sells licensing tools27 to publishers, found that about 30 percent of AI bot visits in late 2025 ignored a site's robots.txt. By October 2026 there were 157 copyright suits against AI companies28 in the United States. The largest result so far, Anthropic's $1.5 billion settlement with book authors29, turned on pirated books rather than crawled web pages, so the courts have yet to say what a robots.txt file is worth.

Evidence: strong for news sites, weak for the web as a whole. Most of the million largest sites still let AI crawlers in, and a robots.txt file is a request, not a lock.

How much of the web do machines now write?

The best-known number about the synthetic flood is a guess. So where did it come from? A 2022 report by Europol's Innovation Lab30 said that experts estimated as much as 90 percent of online content may be synthetically generated by 2026. Its only source was a 2020 book by Nina Schick31, a writer on deepfakes, who said in a 2023 interview that the figure “might” be reached by 2025. No survey, model or measurement stood behind it. It is still often quoted as a Europol forecast.

The measured share is lower, and more interesting. Graphite, the agency that studied search traffic, ran a sample of new English-language articles32 through three commercial AI detectors. It found that 49.9 percent were mostly machine-written in early 2026, a share that has stayed near half for five quarters. In mid-2025 such pages made up only 14 percent of Google's results33 and 7 percent of its first results. NewsGuard, which rates news sites34, counted 49 websites publishing AI-generated news with little human oversight in May 2023 and 3,749 by June 2026, about 77 times as many (a lower bound, since it counts only the sites it has found).

The flood also runs back into the machines. In 2024 Ilia Shumailov and colleagues35 showed in Nature that models trained again and again on their own output lose the rare cases first, and then degrade. In the same paper, keeping some of the original human-written data in each round prevented most of the damage. A web that keeps its record of human writing is worth more to AI companies, not less.

Evidence: moderate. Both measured shares come from one agency's samples of Common Crawl, which over-represents low-value pages, and from commercial detectors that misread a small fraction of human text as machine-written.

Or does the web adapt, as it did before?

The quietest forecast is that the web absorbs AI as it absorbed search engines, social networks and phones. Google's business supports it. Search revenue reached $63.35 billion in the third quarter of 2026, and google.com received 88.4 billion visits in October 2026 against 6 billion for chatgpt.com, about 15 times as many, on Similarweb's estimates36 for the web alone (chatbot apps, where much chatbot use happens, are left out). Across six countries surveyed by the Reuters Institute37, weekly use of standalone AI tools rose from 34 to 51 percent of online adults in a year, and across nine, 57 percent had seen AI answers inside a search engine in the previous week. On these figures, people use chatbots in addition to search, not instead of it.

But adaptation describes the gatekeepers better than the web behind them. Google keeps its users by answering more of their questions itself. The sites that supplied the answers see fewer of those users. AI tools have not filled the gap: they sent under 1 percent of publishers' page views in Chartbeat's data to early 2026.

Evidence: strong that search remains the web's front door; weak that the sites behind it keep their traffic.

What do forecasters expect?

Experts asked about AI in general are divided, and neither survey below asked about the web. Elon University canvassed38 301 experts at the turn of 2025, not a random sample, on how AI would change being human by 2035: 16 percent expected mostly better, 23 percent mostly worse, and 50 percent an equal measure of both. In a 2024 Pew survey39, 56 percent of AI experts expected AI to have a positive effect on the United States over the next 20 years, against 17 percent of the public. What would settle it?

Three numbers will show which future is arriving. If AI tools start to send publishers visits on the scale search does, against under 1 percent of page views now, the hollowed web is being repaired. If agents move from browsing to buying, with well over the 1.9 percent of their requests that now reach a checkout ending in a purchase, the agentic web has begun. And if the AI share of what Google shows climbs from 14 percent toward the 49.9 percent of what is published, the flood has reached the front page. So far the web has kept its searchers, and fewer of their searches end on it.

Method

Figures. Every number on this page is copied from the report, filing or survey cited beside it, as read on 14 November 2026, and listed with its source in the download. Where sources disagree, the page gives the measure closest to the claim and names it. Zero-click shares, for example, run from about a quarter of searches (27.2 percent on a desktop-only panel in March 202540) to about two-thirds (SparkToro's 68.01 percent), depending on which devices and which searches are counted. Ratios and growth rates are computed from the published values by the build script, and rounded half up.

The map. The axes run from 0 to 100 percent. Across is the share of web requests made by software, which today's marker takes from Imperva's 2025 data; Cloudflare's share of non-human requests for web pages, on one day in December 2025 and by a different definition, is similar. Up is the share of what readers are shown that machines wrote, which today's marker takes from Graphite's sample of Google results in mid-2025; the second, smaller square is the AI share of new English articles in early 2026, from the same agency. The placement of each future is this page's judgment of where its predictors put the end state, and each is placed at or beyond today on both axes. The strip is the share of sites that block AI training crawlers in two samples that measure different things.

Limits

Almost every measure of web traffic comes from a company with something to sell: bot protection, search marketing, licensing or analytics. Their samples are their own customers, and their definitions of a bot, a click or an AI-written article differ. AI detectors misclassify some human writing and miss some machine writing, and none of them measures text that a person drafted with AI and then edited. Requests are not readers: one person's question can send an agent to dozens of pages, so the map's horizontal axis overstates how much of the web machines read. The forecasts in the table were picked because each named a number and a date; most predictions about the web name neither.
