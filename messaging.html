Defaults moved low-carbon choices by up to 62 percentage points; labels and signs, by under two

Most advice on climate behavior is advice about words: the right message, the right messenger, the right frame. The field trials point somewhere less glamorous. When researchers counted what people bought or signed up for, the best-tested labels and signs moved the share choosing the low-carbon option by a point or two. Changing what people get if they do nothing moved it by tens of points. That matters to anyone who writes a menu, a tariff or a form, and to anyone weighing how much to expect from a better slogan. This page puts the trials on one scale, shows how message effects shrink as samples grow, and ends with a kit of compromise asks, each graded by how well it has held up.

This data does not update.

  1. In field trials that counted real purchases, carbon labels, a social-norm sign and a renamed dish changed the share of people choosing the low-carbon option by 1.7 percentage points or less.
  2. Making the low-carbon option the default, or putting more of it on the menu, changed the same share by 7.8 to 62 points, for lunches and for electricity tariffs alike.
  3. Those large shifts are in one choice at one counter or form; whether people eat or emit less over a whole day is mostly unmeasured, and the kit below is graded with that in mind.

One scale for unlike trials

Each row of the chart above is a field trial in which people made a real choice, a meal bought or a tariff signed, and the researchers counted the share who chose the lower-carbon option with and without a change. The difference between those two shares is the effect, in percentage points: the gap between two percentages, not a percent of either. A worked example shows the arithmetic. In a German energy supplier's online shop10 in 2012, 7.2 percent of the contracts sold were green when customers had to select the green tariff themselves, and 69.1 percent when it came selected and they had to switch it off. That is 61.9 percentage points, about a tenfold rise, for a green premium of 0.3 euro cents a kilowatt-hour.

The rule for a row is strict on purpose. Surveys of what people say they would choose are left out, because stated intentions run ahead of purchases; so are laboratory games. Home energy reports, which cut electricity use11 by about 2 percent, are left out of this chart for a different reason: they measure how much people use, not the share who chose something, and the two cannot share an axis. They return in the next section. What remains is a small, uneven set, and the gap between its two halves is still too wide for the unevenness to close.

Evidence: strong for the electricity row, a randomized trial with tens of thousands of households; moderate for the food rows, several of which compare weeks or sites rather than randomized people.

Why does the default win?

A default works on three things at once: the effort of changing, the hint that the offered option is the normal one, and plain inattention. None of them asks anyone to be persuaded, which is also why a default is weak evidence of conviction. The Sacramento pricing trial12 shows both sides. It offered households a plan with higher prices at the evening peak, either as a choice to opt into or as a default to opt out of. Households that stayed on the plan only because it was the default cut their peak use by about a fifth as much, per household, as those who chose it. Because about five times as large a share of households ended up on the plan, the default still saved more per household offered it.

The default also lasted. By September 2013, after a second summer, 92.6 percent of the defaulted households were still on the plan, against 18.1 percent of those invited to join. Outside randomized trials the pattern holds over years. When a Swiss utility switched its customers13 to a green tariff unless they objected, about 80 percent of households were still on it four years later. That is a before-and-after case, not an experiment. The message effects followed longest, those of home energy reports, also persist for years, but at about 2 percent of electricity use.

Message effects shrink when the sample grows

The small numbers for words are not an accident of the trials chosen. The effects in studies of behavior change shrink in a predictable way when the studies are repeated at scale. Nudge trials published in academic journals14 reported an average gain of 8.7 percentage points in take-up; the same kinds of nudges run by government behavioral-science units across more than 23 million people averaged 1.4. The authors attribute most of the gap to publication bias, the tendency of small studies with large effects to reach print. Home energy reports kept about three quarters of their effect, 1.71 percent of electricity use falling to 1.31, when they spread from the first 10 sites to 11115, partly because the first sites were the most receptive.

Defaults shrink too, which is the honest part of the chart. A 2022 reanalysis of more than 400 nudge effects17 corrected them for publication bias. Nudges that add information, such as labels and facts, then had no detectable average effect. Nudges that change the choice itself, defaults and menus among them, kept a small one, d 0.58 before correction and 0.12 after, and the authors call that evidence undecided. For climate field interventions as a group18, the bias-corrected effect was d 0.18; in the 32 studies large enough to detect a small effect it was 0.04, about 2 percentage points. The default rows in the hero chart have not been through any such correction, and the smaller and before-and-after studies among them may shrink as well.

Evidence: strong. Three meta-analyses (one of which pools another), a 111-site scale-up of home energy reports and a census of two government nudge units' trials agree on the direction; they differ on how much survives.

What happened when 11 messages were tested in 63 countries?

None of them raised the one costly act the study measured. The test, one of the largest of climate messages, ran online in 2022 and 2023. A team of more than 200 researchers tested 11 interventions chosen by experts19, from a letter to future generations to a summary of the scientific consensus, against a control group that read a passage from Dickens. This page re-estimates the effects from the 59,392 participants in the authors' public data. The message that did most for belief in climate change, decreasing psychological distance, raised it by 3.2 points on a 100-point scale; the one that did most for support of climate policy, exposure to effective collective action, raised that by 2.9. A message designed to stir negative emotion raised willingness to share climate information on social media by 9.8 percentage points, from a base of about 48 percent.

The authors' own model gives the best messages 2.3 points for belief, 2.6 for policy support and 12.1 percentage points for sharing, and ranks the letter to future generations first for policy support, a near tie on this page's estimate. The costly act was a tedious screening task of up to eight pages; for each page completed, the researchers planted one tree. In the control group about 83 percent did at least one page, and the average was 5.0 pages. Six of the 11 messages lowered that number by an amount the data can tell from zero, and the four largest drops, 0.39 to 0.52 pages, are about a tenth of the control average.

The negative-emotion message that did most for sharing did most harm to effort. Part of the loss may be fatigue: several messages took minutes to read or write, and when the authors adjusted for the time spent, five of the shorter messages came out ahead of the control.

Evidence: moderate. The sample is large and the study was preregistered, but every outcome is online and immediate, and the tree task measures effort, not emissions.

What a default does not do

The case against reading the hero chart as a verdict is strong, and it starts with what the long bars count. Each is one choice at one counter, form or shop. A diner served lentils at lunch can order steak at dinner, and most food trials cannot see it. One cafeteria study that looked found no detectable rebound at dinner in the same cafeteria. A Swiss campus study found that sales of animal-based meals20 rose a little the day after meat-free days. No default or menu trial has measured what people ate across a whole day. A 2025 review of rigorous trials aimed at cutting meat21 found an average effect of 0.07 standard deviations on what people ate, and 0.01 after correcting for publication bias. Only two choice-design studies met its standard, because most measured choices on the spot, or hypothetical ones.

Electricity has its own version of the problem. A green tariff moves a customer's contract, but critics argue that it often reallocates22 renewable power that already exists rather than causing new plants to be built. And a default can cost the people least able to notice it: in a Swiss study, about half of the lowest-income23 customers on a green default said they would rather have a cheaper tariff.

The most studied side effect concerns policy. In one online experiment, offering people a green-energy default lowered their support for a carbon tax24 from 70.3 percent to 55.0 percent, as if the small step had paid for the large one. In a later study by the same team, where the default lowered support from 70 to 60 percent, telling people plainly that the default's effect is small, and that most emissions come from elsewhere, raised support for the tax to 72 percent without reducing support for the default. These results come from one research group and hypothetical votes. They are still the best evidence on the question, and they shape the kit below.

Evidence: moderate. Compensation (eating more meat later) and crowding out (a small step lowering support for a large one) are each measured in a handful of studies, mostly short or hypothetical; the direction is consistent, the size is not settled.

A kit built on less, not none

What would a compromise message look like if it were built from this record rather than from intuition? It would change the choice more than the person, offer a smaller step rather than demand a total one, swap rather than subtract, and say honestly how large the step is. The table records what was tested and how much it moved; it is not a ranking of what anyone ought to do. Grade A means several field trials or one large program on real behavior; B, one good field study; C, surveys, online tasks or a single small trial. This page found no field test of the words "sacrifice" or "eco" themselves, so they get no grade.

Read down the grades and a pattern appears. The A rows change the setting: what is offered, what is preselected, what is made cheap or easy, what is shown at the moment of use. The C rows are the ones people argue about most: the wording, the framing, the tone. Persuasion is not useless; it may be what makes a default acceptable to the people who must approve it. But in the record so far, the strongest message is not a sentence. It is the box already checked.

Method

Field trials. A trial enters the hero chart when it meets four rules. It measures a real choice in the field. Its outcome is the share of people or purchases that took the lower-carbon option. It prints the starting share and the effect. Its primary text was opened and read. The build file names the table or page for each row. Where a paper prints both shares, the chart shows them. Where a paper prints only the starting share and an estimated effect, the filled dot is their sum. This applies to the two carbon-label rows and the norm-screen row. The number on each row is the paper's printed effect. The build stops when the difference between the two shares is more than 0.5 percentage points from that effect.

Replication pairs. Each bar in the second chart is a later or larger-sample estimate divided by the first estimate. Both estimates use the paper's own unit. The unit is percentage points, percent of electricity use or Cohen's d. Cohen's d is a difference measured in standard deviations. The build file names the table or page for each value.

Tournament. The data are the authors' deposit on Zenodo. The fetch script checks the file against the checksum that Zenodo publishes. The build drops rows with no condition label. The authors' code does the same. Belief and policy support are each participant's mean over the answered items, 0 to 100. Sharing is 1 if the participant is willing and 0 if not. The build excludes people who do not use social media. The tree task is pages completed, 0 to 8. Each effect is an ordinary least squares estimate against the control group, with country fixed effects and standard errors clustered by country. This model is simpler than the authors' preregistered models. The build stops when any tree-task effect is more than 0.06 pages from the paper's own estimate in its supplementary Table S4.

Grades and dates. The grades are this page's judgment. The kit section defines them. The literature search ends in November 2026.

Limits

The 10 rows in the first chart are a selection, not a census. They are the trials that met the rules above. Other trials of the same kinds give other numbers. Baselines differ: a share of 2 percent and a share of 50 percent cannot move the same way. Several food rows compare weeks or sites, not randomized people. In one of the three dining halls in the plant-based default trial, the default had no effect. That row uses the research team's published summary, because the journal article was not open to read. The page does not convert effects to tonnes of carbon dioxide. A share of choices does not convert to tonnes without assumptions about what people did instead. The tournament measured all outcomes online and immediately. The kit's examples illustrate the tested asks. They are not the wording that the trials used.

The code

Python: the fetch script, which downloads the tournament data and checks it against Zenodo's published checksum; the reader; the payload builder, which re-estimates the tournament and checks every transcribed row against the effect its paper prints; the page generator with the three charts; and the tests. The transcribed tables are in the archive, with the page and table for each row. The tournament data are not in the archive; the fetch script downloads them. The archive is 36 KB.
