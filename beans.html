Nitrogen-fixing beans

A legume makes its own fertiliser. Industry makes the same molecule with a furnace. The question is whether the first can be moved to where the second is needed.

A bean does not need nitrogen fertiliser because it hires. Bacteria live inside nodules on its roots, break the triple bond in atmospheric N2, and hand back ammonia; the plant pays in sugar. A maize plant cannot do this, so its nitrogen arrives from a Haber–Bosch plant instead. Moving that ability — into cereals, or simply further within legumes — is one of the oldest ambitions in plant science, and the gene-editing tools finally make parts of it tractable. This page lays out what is actually working, what is not, and which of the two is which.

The state of it

Four routes, unequal progress

Editing a legume’s own regulation is working now. Giving a cereal the symbiosis has produced structures but no fixation. Putting the nitrogenase genes into a plant directly has produced proteins but no plant. Engineering the bacteria instead is on sale and contested. The figure below states each one at the level it has actually reached.

What the nodule is doing

The chemistry is unforgiving. Atmospheric nitrogen is two atoms held by a triple bond, which is why it is inert and why it is 78% of the air — nothing consumes it easily. Nitrogenase, the bacterial enzyme, breaks it at ambient temperature and pressure, but the arithmetic is brutal: the canonical stoichiometry is sixteen ATP per molecule1 of N2, and a hydrogen molecule is produced as an obligate by-product, so some of the energy is spent on nothing the plant wanted.

The plant pays this bill in fixed carbon — commonly quoted at three to six grams of carbon per gram of nitrogen fixed, depending on species and on whether nodule growth and maintenance are counted alongside the reaction itself. That is the honest frame for the whole field: biological nitrogen fixation is not free nitrogen, it is nitrogen bought with photosynthate. A cereal engineered to fix its own nitrogen would pay the same bill, out of the same account that currently funds grain.

And there is a physical conflict at the centre of it. Nitrogenase is destroyed by oxygen, while the bacteria running it need oxygen to make the ATP it consumes. The nodule solves this twice over: a variable diffusion barrier in the inner cortex controls how much oxygen enters at all, and leghaemoglobin buffers what does, holding free oxygen in the nanomolar range while still ferrying it to respiration. That pairing — a structure and a protein, not a protein alone — is why the symbiosis is an anatomical organ rather than a gene you can paste somewhere else.

The four routes

A. Edit the legume’s own regulation

A legume deliberately limits how many nodules it builds. Roots send CLE peptides to the shoot, which answers through a receptor kinase — SUNN in Medicago, NARK in soybean — and suppresses further nodulation. Soil nitrate feeds into the same cap rather than a second one: nitrate-response regulators of the NLP family switch on CLE peptides that run through the same SUNN/NARK circuit, because fixing nitrogen you could simply absorb is a waste of sugar. One cap, two inputs, and both are editable.

This is where the work is furthest along, and where common bean itself is now a usable subject. Editing Phaseolus vulgaris was hard for years; hairy-root transformation systems combined with CRISPR/Cas9 and in-silico guide prediction have made targeted edits routine enough to answer biological questions. A 2025 study down-regulated two adenine phosphoribosyltransferase2 genes in hairy roots and separated their predominant roles — the two are partly redundant, but one is mostly adenine salvage and the other governs cytokinin homeostasis, with direct consequences for root and nodule growth. That is the shape of current progress: not a super-fixing bean, but the ability to ask precise questions about the machinery that builds nodules.

B. Give cereals the symbiosis

A cereal cannot see rhizobia. The signal exchange that starts nodulation begins with the plant recognising a bacterial Nod factor, and cereals lack the receptors. The workaround is chimeric: fuse the recognition domain of a legume receptor to the signalling domain of a cereal one — MtNFP–ZmMYR1 and MtLYK3–ZmCERK1 in maize, the equivalent constructs on OsMYR1 and OsCERK1 in rice — so the plant perceives the signal and passes it into a pathway it already has, since the symbiotic signalling machinery is shared with the far older mycorrhizal symbiosis. What this produced in rice was calcium spiking in response to the Nod factor: the signal is received. Nothing is built.

Perception is not enough; something has to build the organ. Overexpressing the SHORTROOT–SCARECROW module drives root cortical cell division3 and produces nodule-like structures in Medicago. The same module in rice produces extra cell layers with cortex identity, which is a smaller claim and the honest one. This is real and it is not fixation: no functioning nitrogen-fixing nodule on a cereal has been demonstrated. The gap between a nodule-shaped thing and a nodule is oxygen control, bacterial accommodation, and nutrient exchange, none of which follow automatically from making cells divide.

C. Put the nitrogenase genes into the plant

The most direct route and the hardest. Skip the bacteria: express the nif cluster in the plant itself, targeting mitochondria or chloroplasts because those organelles can supply both the low-oxygen environment nitrogenase requires and the ATP it consumes. The obstacle is that nitrogenase is not one protein but a coordinated assembly of many gene products with metal cofactors that must be built in the right order. Work has reached the stage of expressing individual Nif proteins in plant organelles and assembling parts of the system. No plant fixes nitrogen this way.

D. Engineer the bacteria instead

The commercial route sidesteps the plant. Some free-living and root-associated microbes fix nitrogen already but stop when soil nitrogen is plentiful — which is precisely the condition a fertilised field creates, and precisely where the product is sold; editing that regulation produces microbes that keep fixing under fertiliser. Products of this kind are sold for maize today. The honest summary is that the mechanism is real, the deployment is real, and the size of the per-acre contribution is contested — the claimed nitrogen credits are modest next to a standard application rate, and independent field results vary.

Why the target is worth the difficulty

Ammonia synthesis is one of the largest single chemical sources of carbon dioxide. The IEA puts it at about 450 million tonnes4 of CO2 per year, close to 1.3% of global CO2 emissions, with 72% of production running on natural gas and most of the balance on coal. Ammonia production also accounts for a few per cent of global natural gas use. The range in those numbers is real and reflects different accounting boundaries; what is not in dispute is that the process is large, fossil-fed, and concentrated.

The failure mode of this argument is worth naming. Nitrogen fixed biologically is still nitrogen: it can leach as nitrate and leave fields as nitrous oxide, a greenhouse gas 273 times more potent5 over a century than CO2 per unit mass. A crop that fixes its own nitrogen removes the factory emissions and the transport, not the field emissions. The prize is smaller than “eliminate fertiliser” and still substantial.

What this page is not

This is a synthesis of published work, not original research, and no number here is mine. It states the state of a field as of 2026 and will date. Where a result exists only in a model legume, the page says so; where a structure has formed without function, it says that instead. The distinction between demonstrated, partially demonstrated and proposed is the entire content of the figure, and it is the first thing lost in most coverage of this subject.
