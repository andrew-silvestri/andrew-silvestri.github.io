Night workers sleep in pieces, while the country is awake

Picture an American street at three in the morning. Almost everyone on it is asleep: in the government's time diaries for 2025, 95 percent of people aged 15 and over were asleep at that hour. Among the few who are not are many of the 5.1 million wage and salary workers, 3.4 percent of the total, who usually work a night shift. The usual worry about them is lost sleep, and other measures do find less of it. The diaries show something else as well. Night workers sleep in broken blocks, at hours when about two in three people are awake, and they spend about half of each workday evening asleep or at work. The cost of the night shift is partly a matter of hours, and largely a matter of timing.

  2. In the government's time diaries, night workers sleep in broken blocks, at hours when about two in three people are awake, and spend about half of each workday evening asleep or at work.
  3. Years of night work are associated with modestly more heart disease and diabetes, the cancer evidence is contested, and whether the shift itself causes the excess is not established.

Who works at night

Night work concentrates in jobs that run around the clock. In BLS's 2024–25 tables, 3.4 percent of wage and salary workers usually worked a regular night shift1, and the share reached 11.7 percent in transportation and material moving and 7.9 percent in production occupations. The occupation groups are broad: hospital nurses, for instance, are counted in the professional group. About 61 percent of night workers are men, roughly three in five, against a little over half of all wage and salary workers.

Why do they do it? The survey asked each night worker for the main reason, and the answer most often given was not pay. It was personal preference, at 30.7 percent, ahead of the nature of the job (19.8 percent) and better pay (17.5 percent). The split by sex is wide. Nearly half of women on nights (47.6 percent) gave preference as the reason, against about one in five men (20.0 percent), while pay was named by 22.6 percent of men and 9.4 percent of women. Better arrangements for family or child care accounted for 16.5 percent; for some of them, being home in the evening or handing children over at the door is the point of the schedule. For a large minority, the night shift is a choice.

How do we know what a night worker's day looks like?

The numbers in the clock come from the American Time Use Survey, which the Census Bureau runs for BLS2. Each respondent recounts one randomly chosen day, activity by activity, from 4 a.m. to 4 a.m., with who was present. In 2024 and 2025 the same respondents also answered the Leave and Job Flexibilities Module, which asked which shift they usually work. Joining the two gives what neither publishes: the diary days of 190 usual night workers, 122 of them on a day they worked. Before it uses the files, the build reproduces BLS's own figures: the 3.4 percent share of night workers, the reasons above, and the share of the country asleep at 3 a.m.3 Standard errors come from the survey's 160 replicate weights.

That makes the country's clock well measured and the night workers' clock a small sample, and the table below gives the uncertainty. A second caution concerns the word asleep. The diaries record what people say they were doing, and time in bed trying to sleep counts as sleep. Other measures find less. In a national health survey, 44.0 percent of night workers said they usually slept six hours or less4, against 28.8 percent of day workers. Wrist-worn monitors on Danish night warehouse workers5 recorded 5 hours 39 minutes of sleep after night shifts and 7 hours 24 minutes on recovery days. The diaries are best read for when people sleep and how the sleep is broken, not for its exact length.

Read that way, the workday difference in reported sleep is small: night workers report 21 minutes more than day workers, with a standard error of 16, a difference the sample cannot tell from zero. The difference in shape is large. Night workers' longest sleep is about an hour shorter, and they are about four times as likely to sleep in two or more blocks.

The body clock rarely follows

The timing matters because the body keeps its own schedule. In 170 healthy adults aged 18 to 45, salivary melatonin, the hormone that marks the biological night, rose at 20:50 on average6 and fell at 08:21. Core body temperature reaches its daily low7 around 05:00, close to the middle of a normal night's sleep, and the early-morning hours are when sleepiness peaks and attention fails most8. A night worker is at work through that low point and in bed during the hours the body treats as day. Sleep at that hour runs against the clock, and measured day sleep is shorter.

Does the clock move to fit the job? Rarely, and not fully. A 2008 review of six studies9 of permanent night workers found that fewer than 3 percent adjusted their melatonin rhythm completely and fewer than one in four adjusted enough to benefit. A 2013 study of police officers10 on rotating shifts, measured after seven consecutive nights, found that 7 of 17 cases adapted while the rest barely moved. On North Sea oil platforms, where crews live11 at the worksite, the clock does shift. Elsewhere, morning daylight, often on the trip home, works against the shift: light at that hour pulls the clock back toward a day schedule, which is why adaptation studies have workers wear dark glasses outdoors.

The metabolic cost of the mismatch has been measured directly, on a small scale. In a laboratory, ten adults12 whose meals and sleep were moved around the clock for ten days showed higher blood glucose despite more insulin, 17 percent lower leptin, a hormone that signals fullness, and in 3 of 8 people a post-meal glucose response in the prediabetic range. That shows a mechanism, not a disease.

What years of nights are associated with

The long-term evidence comes mostly from large cohorts of nurses who reported how many years they had worked rotating night shifts, at least three nights a month alongside day and evening shifts, which is not the fixed night schedule of the workers in the diaries. In the Nurses' Health Studies, ten or more years of rotating nights were associated with an 18 percent higher risk of coronary heart disease13. Fifteen or more years were associated with a 23 percent higher risk of death from cardiovascular disease14. Twenty or more years went with a 58 percent higher risk of type 2 diabetes15, falling to 24 percent once body weight was taken into account, which suggests that part of the link runs through weight. A 2012 meta-analysis of 34 studies16 and about two million people found a 23 percent higher risk of heart attack among shift workers, though no higher overall death rate.

Cancer is the contested case. In 2019 the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified night shift work as probably carcinogenic to humans, Group 2A17, on limited evidence in humans and stronger evidence from experimental animals. But when ten prospective studies of 1.4 million women were pooled in 2016, the relative risk of breast cancer for night work was 0.9918, with no excess after 20 or 30 years either. Studies that ask women with and without cancer to recall their working lives find higher risks than studies that follow women forward19 in time, which is the pattern recall bias would produce.

How much of this does the shift cause? The studies are observational, and two biases push in opposite directions. Workers who fall ill tend to leave night work20, which hides harm. People with less education and higher body weight are more likely to end up on shifts in the first place, which a 2021 genetic study found could inflate it21. Across the cohorts, the excess risk is modest, on the order of a fifth, and grows with years on nights. That it is caused by the nights is not established.

Accidents and the drive home

The acute risks are clearer. Pooling studies of workplace incidents, Simon Folkard and David Lombardi found22 risk 27.9 percent higher on night shifts than on morning shifts, 36 percent higher on the fourth consecutive night than on the first, and 27.5 percent higher on 12-hour shifts than on 8-hour ones. The commute home is the other exposure. In a study published in 2016, on a closed track23, 16 night workers drove an instrumented car twice, once after a night's sleep and once after a night shift. After sleep there were no near-crashes. After the shift, 6 of the 16 drives had at least one, and 7 were stopped early for safety.

Evenings and family

The clock shows where the social cost falls. Between 18:00 and 22:00, the hours when most people are awake and off work, night workers on workdays are asleep or at work 53 percent of the time, against 20 percent for day workers. Their total time with family or friends on a workday is about the same as day workers', spread over other hours. On days off the diaries show a gap: night workers report 111 fewer minutes with family and friends than day workers do on theirs. With 68 day-off diaries the standard error is 44 minutes, so the true gap could be anywhere from about half an hour to three hours, and the comparison is not adjusted for who the night workers are. They also report 10.8 hours of sleep on days off, against 9.7 for day workers.

Studies of marriages find costs in particular groups rather than across the board. In a sample of 3,476 American couples24 followed for about five years, men with children who were married less than five years and worked fixed nights were some six times as likely to separate or divorce as those on days; among women with children married more than five years, the odds tripled. A later study of newlyweds25 found that wives' fixed night shifts raised divorce risk, but only among couples without children. For children, a 2014 review found adverse associations26 in 21 of 23 studies of parents' evening, night and weekend schedules.

What it pays

No federal law requires a private employer27 in the United States to pay more for nights: the Fair Labor Standards Act leaves extra pay for night work to agreement between employer and employee. The federal government pays its own General Schedule employees a 10 percent differential for regularly scheduled work28 between 18:00 and 06:00. In 13 European countries29, workers who receive a shift premium are paid 5 to 16 percent of gross wages for it. In a Belgian choice experiment with mostly union-member respondents, the median worker asked for 36 percent more an hour to take a fixed night shift instead of a day job. Those already on nights asked for about 8 percent, and day workers for 48 percent. The people who work nights are, in part, the people who mind them least.

Who it can suit

People differ in when their clocks run, and one large study suggests that the mismatch between clock and schedule matters, not only the night itself. Among 64,615 nurses30, early risers' diabetes risk rose with years of night shifts, while late risers had their highest risk on day schedules. Preference also differs: the largest group of night workers chose the shift. In a study of simulated night shifts, bright light during the shift, dark glasses on the way home and a dark bedroom moved young volunteers' melatonin onset31 to about 03:20. That target was a deliberate compromise, late enough to help night work and early enough to leave days off workable. The diaries cannot say whether a night worker sleeps well. They show when the sleep comes, and which hours of the evening the shift claims.

Method

Data. BLS American Time Use Survey public-use files for 2024 and 2025 (respondent, activity, who, summary and roster files) and the Leave and Job Flexibilities Module, 2024–25, with its weights. No diaries were collected in October 2025. The module covers 6,593 employed wage and salary respondents; 190 usually work a night shift. 3,086 usual day workers kept a diary on a day they worked and 2,703 on a day they did not. A workday is a diary day with any time coded as working (activity codes 0501xx). Asleep is activity code 0101xx. Family and friends follow BLS's own per-respondent totals, which the build reproduces from the who-codes; their sum counts a few minutes twice when both are present. Work time is excluded from time with family or friends.

Clock. The diary day starts at 04:00. A person counts as asleep at a time if a sleep activity starts at or before that time and ends after it. The rings use ten-minute steps. The everyone ring uses the diary weights of all respondents in both years, each year scaled to one average day. The night and day rings use the module weights. Sleep blocks are measured in the same ten-minute steps, with the clock read as a circle, so a sleep that runs past 04:00 joins the sleep that starts the diary. The evening share is the share of the 24 ten-minute points from 18:00 to 21:50 spent asleep or at work. The body-clock arc and dot are published averages for healthy adults, not values from these diaries.

Checks. The build stops unless it reproduces, to the published decimal, BLS's share of night workers (3.4 percent), the number of night workers (5,144 thousand), the reasons in Table 8, and Table A-3's shares of people asleep at 03:00 and 07:00 and working at 10:00 in 2025. Standard errors use the 160 replicate weights by successive-difference replication.

Limits

The night sample is 190 people, 122 of them on a workday and 68 on a day off, so the night figures carry standard errors of several points. They are averages over shifts that start at different hours. A night shift crosses the diary's 04:00 boundary, so about 24 percent of night workdays hold only the end of a shift that began the evening before, and a day off that follows a shift would show work before dawn, so the days off are mostly second or later days off. One diary day per person shows a typical day, not a person's week. The comparisons with day workers are raw averages, not adjusted for age, sex or occupation. Reported sleep includes time in bed. The health evidence comes from observational studies, mostly of rotating shifts among nurses, and it cannot fully separate the shift from the people who take it. This page summarizes evidence and is not medical advice.
