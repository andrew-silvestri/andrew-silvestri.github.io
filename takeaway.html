Each new Permian pipeline buys the basin only a year or so

Imagine an oil well in West Texas on a day when every pipe out of the Permian is full. It was drilled for oil, but gas comes up with the oil whether or not there is a pipe to carry it away, as it does from oil wells across West Texas and New Mexico. At the Waha hub, where the basin's gas is traded, the price has fallen below zero, so the well's owner pays to have the gas taken away — and keeps pumping, because the oil is what pays. Pipelines out of the basin arrive every few years, each about the size of one or two years' growth. So each new one holds only a year to a year and a half of the basin's gas growth, and then the well is back where it started.

  1. Since 2017 the Permian has added, on average, 1.6 to 2.0 billion cubic feet a day of gas that needs a pipe out each year, which is close to what one big new pipeline carries. So each big line built since 2019 holds only 12 to 19 months of that growth.
  2. When the pipes have been full, Waha's price has gone below zero and sellers have paid buyers to take their gas. Reuters counts 17 such days in 2019, 49 in 2024 and 87 in 2026 by late May, already more than in any full year.
  3. Flaring has not come back with the full pipes. Operators in the Texas Permian vented or flared 4.1 percent of their gas in 2019. In any year since 2021, they vented or flared no more than 1.6 percent.

Gas grows every year, pipe in steps

That well is the basin in miniature: gas grows every year, and pipe arrives in steps. The chart sets the two side by side, both measured from August 2024, the month EIA reported Waha's price below zero "every day since July 261". One is pipe capacity out of the basin, rising a step each time a line enters service. The other is the gas that needs a pipe out, which is production times k, the share not removed as liquids or burned as fuel in the field and at plants. How big is k? That depends on whose ratio is used, so the chart draws the gas twice — once with the statewide figure that EIA's data give (0.815), and once with the lower share that makes mid-2019, when EIA said the pipes were constrained, as full as August 2024 (0.65). Where the pipe line runs above the gas lines, there is room; where it runs below them, there is more gas than this accounting can place. Beneath the chart sit the days when Waha's price was below zero and the share of gas vented or flared in the basin's three Texas districts.

2018 to mid-2019. Production climbs quickly, and no large line leaves the basin. In September 2019 EIA wrote that limited takeaway kept Waha's prices "very low, or even negative, in recent months", and in 2019 the three Texas districts vented or flared 0.47 Bcf/d (4.1 percent of their gas).

Gulf Coast Express, September 2019. The line carries 1.98 Bcf/d to the Texas coast, and the share vented or flared drops to 2.1 percent in 2020, half the 2019 share. But the 2020 price collapse also stops production growing.

Permian Highway and Whistler, 2021. The two lines add 2.1 and 2.0 Bcf/d, six months apart, while the 2020 slump still holds production down. Reuters lists no below-zero day at Waha in 2021 or 2022.

2023 to Aug 2024. About two Bcf/d of new gas a year fills the room, and by August 2024 Waha is below zero every day. The chart sets the two lines to meet here.

Matterhorn Express, October 2024. The line carries 2.5 Bcf/d, the largest line yet, or about 15 months of growth. The room that it opened was gone by December 2025, and Waha went below zero 39 times in 2025.

2026. Reuters counts 87 below-zero days by May 28, a record. In September 2026, on the default reading, the gap is −0.48 Bcf/d — but on most of the readings below it is within 0.7 Bcf/d of August 2024, on one side or the other.

The two lines are drawn to meet in August 2024, so the chart shows the change in room since then, not how much room there was. The farther it runs from that month, the more the two gas lines separate, which is what the check on 2019 below is about. The below-zero days are Reuters's count of days when Waha's daily average was below zero. A dash marks a year that its list does not name, and 2026 runs to May 28. EIA, from Natural Gas Intelligence data2, counts differently: Waha traded below zero on 42% of trading days in 2024.

Why does the gas keep coming?

Because most of it is a by-product. In 2025, 69 percent of the gas the Railroad Commission recorded in the basin's three districts3 came from oil wells, which are drilled for oil and bring the gas up with it. Reuters put the economics in one sentence: "Energy firms in the Permian have been willing to take some losses on gas because they can make up for those with profits from selling oil." So a low or even negative gas price does not, by itself, stop the oil. An operator can shut a well in, as many did in 2020, but that stops the oil too. When the pipes are full, then, the gas that cannot be moved is sold at whatever Waha pays, or burned or released at the lease. The Commission's own account of flaring says that in existing fields it "may be necessary because existing pipelines may have reached capacity4" — the regulator, in other words, names the same bottleneck this page measures.

And the by-product grows. Since 2017, the gas that needs a pipe out has grown by 1.95 Bcf/d a year at the default share (1.56 at the lowest share this page considers), and every calendar year added at least 1.6 Bcf/d except 2020, when operators reduced activity and it added 0.11. The four big lines built since 2019 each added 1.98 to 2.5 Bcf/d, which is 12 to 19 months of that growth. How long each one actually lasted on the chart is another matter, because the lines did not arrive evenly. Gulf Coast Express took 47 months to fill, over the 2020 slowdown. Permian Highway and Whistler, six months apart, took 20 and 9 months; Matterhorn Express 14.

Full pipes show in the price, not the flare

EIA describes the mechanism: "When regional growth in natural gas production outpaces pipeline takeaway capacity additions, capacity constraints exert downward pressure on spot natural gas prices at the Waha Hub, which is near Permian Basin production." Do the years agree with it? They do. Reuters dates Waha's first below-zero daily average to 2019, the year Gulf Coast Express opened, and counts six in 2020. It lists no such day in 2021 or 2022, when the chart shows at least 2.5 Bcf/d of room every month. It then counts one in 2023, 49 in 2024 as the room disappeared, 39 in 2025 and 87 in 2026 by May 28 — a count that includes a record run of 78 days in a row, which Reuters attributed to "pipeline constraints from spring maintenance5". A capacity list like this one does not contain maintenance, which is one reason the price can turn before the chart does. Negative prices, in Reuters's description, are ones "which force some energy firms to pay others to take gas associated with their oil production". That is the well at the top of this page.

Venting and flaring tell a different story after 2019. In the three Texas districts, 0.47 Bcf/d was vented or flared in 2019 (4.1 percent of the gas produced there). In every year from 2021 to 2025 the share stayed between 1.3 and 1.6 percent, even as gross output at the lease there (the Commission's measure, not EIA's) increased from 13.2 to 19.2 Bcf/d, and even as the below-zero days returned. As the pipes filled again, the share moved only from 1.3 percent in 2022 to 1.6 in 2023 and 1.5 in 2024. The full pipes of 2024 to 2026 show in the price far more than in flaring. Why? The data cannot say. Reuters offers a view, in its own words: "Negative gas prices were not very common a decade ago when environmental rules were less strict and many drillers could flare or burn off some of their unwanted gas."

Each fall in flaring after a new line can also be tested against ordinary months, using the average of the twelve months before and after each start. Around Gulf Coast Express, the share fell from 4.57 to 2.54 percent; around Permian Highway and Whistler, from 2.10 to 1.28; and around the group that ends with Matterhorn, from 1.52 to 1.36. Of 104 before-and-after differences at months clear of any start, none fell as far as the 2019 one, while 9 fell further than the 2021 group's and 19 fell further than the Matterhorn group's. Only the first stands out. Even that does not show that a pipe caused a fall: the 2019 window includes the price collapse and shut-ins of 20206, and the 2021 window includes the February 2021 winter storm7.

How full are the pipes?

No gauge on this page can say directly. In September 2026, on the default reading, the pipes carry 0.48 Bcf/d more gas than this accounting says they had room for in August 2024. That is a comparison with August 2024, when the prices showed that the pipes were full, not a measurement of how full they are now. The number is fragile, too. But the sign changes with the choices, so the page does not say which side they are on: whether the basin has a little room or a little too much gas turns on choices such as the share k, which a single headline number would hide. Two rows go further. One assumes that the pipes were full a month earlier. The other counts the first phase of Hugh Brinson (1.5 Bcf/d) at its full capacity from September. That line was reported in commercial service in August and expected to reach its capacity by September 1, but no source in hand says it did.

A negative reading needs a word of its own. Pipes cannot carry more than their capacity, so a negative reading means that this accounting has missed a place where the gas went: lines that run above their nameplate, capacity added without a project of its own, more gas burned in the basin, or a smaller share that needs a pipe.

Pipes still to come

Is more pipe coming? Plenty, on paper. The tracker and the pipelines' own reports expect 15.73 Bcf/d more by February 2030, of which 8.55 Bcf/d is on lines that the tracker shows under construction or partly complete. The other 7.18 Bcf/d (Permian West Expansion, DeLa Express, Desert Southwest and Saguaro Connector) is on lines that are not yet under construction. Appendix: the check on 2019

The chart uses the same arithmetic back to 2018, and there it shows room that the prices did not. In July 2019, the month before Gulf Coast Express began taking gas8, EIA wrote that takeaway "kept prices very low, or even negative, in recent months". Yet the default reading gives +1.91 Bcf/d of room that month, and EIA's own Permian ratio (k = 0.732) still gives +0.95. A share that makes July 2019 exactly as full as August 2024 is 0.65; it moves to 0.62 without South Mainline, an ambiguous westbound line, and to 0.77 if Double E, which EIA counted as takeaway in 20219, is counted too. That share is too sensitive to print as a measurement. But every version is below the default, and the price evidence leans the same way. With the lower share, the pipes in September 2026 are slightly emptier than in August 2024, not fuller (+0.26 Bcf/d in the table). That is why the page gives no sign.

Method

Production. The source is EIA's Short-Term Energy Outlook, October 2026 release, Table 5a, series NGMPPM10: marketed natural gas, Permian region, monthly, history to September 2026. The page does not use its forecast months, and its latest months are estimates. The region is drawn by county11, 51 in Texas and 4 in New Mexico. The workbook does not reach the months before January 2022, so for those months the chart uses EIA's last Drilling Productivity Report (total gas, the same counties) times 0.974. This is the mean ratio of the two over the 18 months they share (range 0.951 to 0.988). The ratio increases over those months, so, applied to 2018 and 2019, when more gas was flared, it slightly overstates production in those years and understates the 2019 discrepancy. It is fixed at that value, and the monthly refresh cannot change it. The Railroad Commission's Permian figures (districts 7C, 08 and 8A, Texas only, measured at the lease) were checked against EIA's over 49 months, and the ratio stayed between 0.68 and 0.77.

The share k. Not every unit of marketed gas leaves by pipe: liquids are removed, and some gas is burned in the field and at plants. EIA gives both by state12. For 2024, dry gas less lease and plant fuel was 0.815 of marketed production across Texas and New Mexico; this is the default. For Texas alone it was 0.792, and for dry gas alone 0.857. These are statewide ratios standing in for the Permian. EIA's Permian-only figures (STEO Table 10b, shale formations, over Table 5a) give 0.774, or 0.732 less fuel, but that ratio is biased low, because the formations table does not include conventional gas.

Capacity. A line counts when it starts in the basin and a cited source says that it carries gas out of the Permian; lines inside the basin, lines that start downstream, and laterals do not. Capacity and dates come from EIA's project tracker13, by exact name. Where a newer report gives a date14, the page uses that date and names the report, and where sources disagree on a start, it uses the later month. A line is in service only where an opened source says so. Before May 2022, the chart counts the lines in the second table, by the same rule; South Mainline and Permian North, westbound with no note in the tracker, are the ambiguous ones. The arithmetic for room uses only changes, so the level of capacity never enters it.

Limits

The production figure is an estimate, and EIA revises its latest months. Capacity is nameplate and enters as a full step, but lines increase their flow gradually and maintenance removes capacity, so prices can go below zero while the chart still shows room. The reference month is an inference from prices. The share k is a statewide ratio, and the check on 2019 says it is probably too high for this purpose. Venting and flaring are what operators report at the lease; the page does not count flaring at gathering systems and plants (Form R-3). Several in-service dates come from trade press reports of what the pipelines' owners said. The page forecasts nothing and names no company as the cause of anything.
