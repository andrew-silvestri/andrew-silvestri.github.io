Each new pipeline out of the Permian holds only a year to a year and a half of the basin's gas growth, and when the pipes are full, sellers pay to get rid of the gas

Oil wells in West Texas and New Mexico bring gas up with the oil whether or not there is a pipe to carry it away. Pipelines out of the basin arrive every few years, each about the size of one or two years' growth, and when they are full the price at the Waha hub, where the basin's gas is traded, falls below zero: producers pay to have their gas taken away.

  1. Since 2017 the Permian has added, on average, 1.6 to 2.0 billion cubic feet a day of gas that needs a pipe out each year, close to what one big new pipeline carries, so each one built since 2019 holds only 12 to 19 months of that growth.
  2. When the pipes have been full, Waha's price has gone below zero and sellers have paid buyers to take their gas: Reuters counts 17 such days in 2019, 49 in 2024 and 87 in 2026 by late May, more than in any full year.
  3. Burning the gas off has not come back with the full pipes: operators in the Texas Permian vented or flared 4.1 per cent of their gas in 2019 and no more than 1.6 per cent in any year since 2021.

Gas grows every year; pipe arrives in steps

The chart sets two things against each other, both measured from August 2024, the month EIA reported Waha's price below zero "every day since July 261". One is pipe capacity out of the basin; each step is a line entering service. The other is the gas that needs a pipe out: production, times the share of it (k) that is not taken out as liquids or burned as fuel in the field and at plants. That share is uncertain, so the gas is drawn twice: at the statewide figure EIA's data give (0.815), and at the lower share that makes mid-2019, when EIA said the pipes were constrained, as full as August 2024 (0.65). Where the pipe line is above the gas lines there is room; where it is below them, there is more gas than this accounting can place. Underneath are the days Waha's price went below zero and the share of gas vented or flared in the basin's three Texas districts.

2018 to mid-2019. Production rises fast and no large line leaves the basin. EIA wrote in September 2019 that limited takeaway had kept Waha's prices "very low, or even negative, in recent months". In 2019 the three Texas districts vented or flared 0.47 Bcf/d, 4.1 per cent of their gas.

Gulf Coast Express, September 2019. 1.98 Bcf/d to the Texas coast. The share vented or flared is 2.1 per cent in 2020, half 2019's; 2020's price collapse stalls production too.

Permian Highway and Whistler, 2021. 2.1 and 2.0 Bcf/d, six months apart, while 2020's slump still held production back. Reuters lists no below-zero day at Waha in 2021 or 2022.

2023 to Aug 2024. About two Bcf/d of new gas a year uses the room up. By August 2024 Waha is below zero every day; the chart is lined up so the two lines meet here.

Matterhorn Express, October 2024. 2.5 Bcf/d, the largest line yet, about 15 months of growth. The room it opened was gone by December 2025. Waha went below zero 39 times in 2025.

2026. 87 below-zero days by May 28, a record. In September 2026, on the default reading, the gap sits at −0.48 Bcf/d: within 0.7 Bcf/d of August 2024 on most of the readings below.

The two lines are drawn to meet in August 2024: the chart shows the change in room since then, not how much room there was. The further it runs from that month, the more the two gas lines part, which is the subject of the check on 2019 below. Below-zero days are Reuters's count of days Waha's daily average was below zero; a dash marks a year its list does not name, and 2026 runs to May 28. EIA, from Natural Gas Intelligence data2, counts differently: Waha traded below zero on 42% of trading days in 2024.

Why the gas keeps coming

In 2025, 69 per cent of the gas the Railroad Commission recorded in the basin's three districts3 came from oil wells. Those wells are drilled for oil, and the gas comes up with it. Reuters put the economics in one sentence: "Energy firms in the Permian have been willing to take some losses on gas because they can make up for those with profits from selling oil." A low or negative gas price does not, by itself, stop the oil. An operator can shut a well in, as many did in 2020, but that stops the oil too. When the pipes are full, the gas that cannot be moved is sold at whatever Waha pays, or burned or released at the lease; the Commission's own account of flaring says that in existing fields it "may be necessary because existing pipelines may have reached capacity4".

Since 2017, the gas that needs a pipe out has grown by 1.95 Bcf/d a year at the default share, or 1.56 at the lowest share this page considers. Every calendar year added at least 1.6 Bcf/d except 2020, when operators cut back and it added 0.11. The four big lines built since 2019 each added 1.98 to 2.5 Bcf/d, which is 12 to 19 months of that growth. How long each actually lasted on the chart differs, because the lines did not arrive evenly: Gulf Coast Express took 47 months to be used up, over the 2020 slowdown; Permian Highway and Whistler, six months apart, 20 and 9; Matterhorn Express 14.

Prices and flaring

EIA describes the mechanism: "When regional growth in natural gas production outpaces pipeline takeaway capacity additions, capacity constraints exert downward pressure on spot natural gas prices at the Waha Hub, which is near Permian Basin production." The years line up with it. Reuters dates Waha's first below-zero daily average to 2019, the year Gulf Coast Express opened, and counts six in 2020; it lists no such day in 2021 or 2022, when the chart shows at least 2.5 Bcf/d of room every month; then one in 2023, 49 in 2024 as the room ran out, 39 in 2025 and 87 in 2026 by May 28, including a record run of 78 days in a row that it put down to "pipeline constraints from spring maintenance5", which a capacity list like this one does not contain. Reuters describes negative prices as ones "which force some energy firms to pay others to take gas associated with their oil production".

Venting and flaring tell a different story after 2019. In the three Texas districts, 0.47 Bcf/d was vented or flared in 2019, 4.1 per cent of the gas produced there. In every year from 2021 to 2025 it stayed between 1.3 and 1.6 per cent, even as gross output at the lease there (the Commission's measure, not EIA's) rose from 13.2 to 19.2 Bcf/d and the below-zero days returned. As the pipes refilled the share went from 1.3 per cent in 2022 to 1.6 in 2023 and 1.5 in 2024. The full pipes of 2024 to 2026 show in the price far more than in flaring; the data cannot say why. Reuters offers a view, in its own words: "Negative gas prices were not very common a decade ago when environmental rules were less strict and many drillers could flare or burn off some of their unwanted gas."

Each fall in flaring after a new line can also be tested against ordinary months. Averaged over the twelve months before and after, the share fell from 4.57 to 2.54 per cent around Gulf Coast Express, from 2.10 to 1.28 around Permian Highway and Whistler, and from 1.52 to 1.36 around the group ending with Matterhorn. Of 104 before-and-after differences at months clear of any start, none fell as far as the 2019 one; 9 fell further than the 2021 group's and 19 further than the Matterhorn group's. Only the first stands out. None of this shows that a pipe caused a fall: the 2019 window runs into the price collapse and shut-ins of 20206, and the 2021 window takes in the February 2021 winter storm7.

How full the pipes are now

In September 2026, on the default reading, the pipes carry 0.48 Bcf/d more gas than this accounting says they had room for in August 2024. Appendix: the check on 2019

The chart runs back to 2018 on the same arithmetic, and there it shows room the prices did not. In July 2019, the month before Gulf Coast Express began taking gas8, EIA wrote that takeaway "kept prices very low, or even negative, in recent months". The default reading gives +1.91 Bcf/d of room that month; with EIA's own Permian ratio (k = 0.732) it gives +0.95. A share that makes July 2019 exactly as full as August 2024 is 0.65; it moves to 0.62 without South Mainline, an ambiguous westbound line, and to 0.77 if Double E, which EIA counted as takeaway in 20219, is counted too. That is too sensitive to print as a measurement, but every version is below the default, and the price evidence leans the same way. With the lower share the pipes in September 2026 come out slightly emptier than in August 2024 rather than fuller (+0.26 Bcf/d in the table), which is why the page gives no sign.

Method

Production. EIA's Short-Term Energy Outlook, October 2026 release, Table 5a, series NGMPPM10: marketed natural gas, Permian region, monthly, history to September 2026; its forecast months are not used, and its latest months are estimates. The region is drawn by county11, 51 in Texas and 4 in New Mexico. Before January 2022, which the workbook does not reach, the chart uses EIA's last Drilling Productivity Report (total gas, the same counties) times 0.974, the mean ratio of the two over the 18 months they share (range 0.951 to 0.988). The ratio drifts up over those months, so applying it to 2018 and 2019, when more gas was flared, slightly overstates production then and understates the 2019 discrepancy. The ratio is fixed at that value so the monthly refresh cannot move it. The Railroad Commission's Permian figures (districts 7C, 08 and 8A, Texas only, measured at the lease) were checked against EIA's over 49 months; the ratio held between 0.68 and 0.77.

The share k. Not every unit of marketed gas leaves by pipe: liquids are taken out, and some gas is burned in the field and at plants. EIA gives both by state12; for 2024, dry gas less lease and plant fuel was 0.815 of marketed production across Texas and New Mexico, the default; Texas alone 0.792, and dry gas alone 0.857. These are statewide ratios standing in for the Permian. EIA's Permian-only figures (STEO Table 10b, shale formations, over Table 5a) give 0.774, or 0.732 less fuel; that ratio is biased low, because the formations table leaves out conventional gas.

Capacity. A line counts when it starts in the basin and a cited source describes it as carrying gas out of the Permian; lines inside the basin, lines that start downstream, and laterals do not. Capacity and dates are read from EIA's project tracker13 by exact name; where a newer report gives a date14, it is used and named, and where sources disagree on a start, the later month is used. A line is in service only where an opened source says so. Before May 2022 the chart counts the lines in the second table, by the same rule; South Mainline and Permian North, westbound with no note in the tracker, are the ambiguous ones. The headroom arithmetic uses changes only, so the level of capacity never enters it.

Venting and flaring. The Railroad Commission's Production Data Query dump15, extracted 18 September 2026: gas reported vented or flared (disposition code 4, which does not separate the two) over gas-well and casinghead production, districts 7C, 08 and 8A. The six most recent months are left out because late reports still change them. Before-and-after windows are twelve months each; starts less than twelve months apart are taken together; a start is a line of at least 0.5 Bcf/d.

Limits

The production figure is an estimate and EIA revises its latest months. Capacity is nameplate and enters as a full step, while lines ramp up and maintenance takes capacity out, so prices can go below zero while the chart still shows room. The reference month is an inference from prices. The share k is a statewide ratio and the check on 2019 says it is probably too high for this purpose. Venting and flaring are what operators report at the lease; flaring at gathering systems and plants (Form R-3) is not counted. Several in-service dates rest on trade press reporting what the pipelines' owners said. The page forecasts nothing and names no company as the cause of anything.
