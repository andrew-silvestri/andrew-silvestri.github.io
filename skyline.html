Skylines, played

A skyline and a spectrum analyser are the same picture. So the picture is played.

Both are a row of vertical bars of varying height, both are read left to right, and in both the eye goes straight to the tall spikes. That is a coincidence of drawing conventions rather than of physics, but it is a usable one: if a skyline can be read as a spectrum, a city can be turned into a sound and the sound will change as you turn around.

What is measured and what is invented

Sonification projects usually blur this line, so it is worth drawing sharply.

Measured. Every building is a real entity with a published structural height and a published coordinate. The observer stands at a stated point outside the city, and the angular position and apparent height of each tower from that point is trigonometry on those two numbers and nothing else. The silhouette is a genuine one: where a tower stands behind a taller tower it contributes nothing, which is what your eye does.

Assumed. Footprint width is not recorded anywhere usable, so each tower is given a width of eighteen per cent of its height, between twenty-five and seventy metres. That is about right for a slender tower and about wrong for a broad one.

Rearranged. The spacing around the wheel, and only the spacing. Order, height, distance and bearing are all real; how far apart two towers sit on the ring is not. This is set out in full below rather than buried, because it is the one place the picture departs from the ground.

Invented. The sound. There is no sense in which a city has a pitch. The mapping is a choice, it is written out in full below, and it is deterministic: the same city at the same bearing always makes the same sound.

Two panels, not one picture

Above is a wheel, drawn the way a reverb room or a rotary control is drawn: a cylinder seen from slightly above, with each building a stave standing on the rim. Screen position comes from the sine of its angle and depth from the cosine, so the near face is large, bright and low, and the far face — the part of the city behind you — rides higher, smaller and in a colder colour. It is drawn back to front, so near towers hide far ones for free. Below is the same ring as a meter, with fast attack, slow release and peak markers that fall back under their own weight, which is how every meter ever built behaves.

Turning does not rotate the city. It rotates you. What was behind comes around, and the colour tells you when it has.

One liberty, taken deliberately

The first version put every tower at its true bearing from a viewpoint outside the city. That is exactly what a skyline is, it is entirely honest, and as a carousel it was useless: a downtown occupies perhaps forty degrees of anybody's horizon, so turning ninety degrees gave a blank screen. Accurate and worthless is still worthless.

So the wheel does one stated thing to the geography. Buildings keep their true compass order — clockwise from north, exactly as they stand on the ground, and the panel names the real bearing of anything you hover — but their spacing is equalised so the ring is full. Neighbours on the wheel are neighbours in the city; the angle between them is not the angle between them. It is the liberty a transit map takes with distance, taken for the same reason. Every one of the fifty cities now has something in front of you at every bearing, which was checked rather than assumed.

Two other things changed with it. The height floor came down: 120 m for the headline count, 55 m for wheel membership, which roughly doubles the set. And the wheel carries up to 120 buildings per city rather than 20 — 1,115 across the whole set, New York fullest at 120.

It also cost me twenty-six cities, and that is the honest part. The ask was the top fifty; the database supports 24. Beijing has seven towers on record here and something over a thousand in life, and a seven-tower wheel would have said more about Wikidata's coverage than about Beijing while reading, to anybody looking at it, as a statement about Beijing. A city is published when it has enough buildings to fill a turn. Padding the list back to fifty would have meant putting the original complaint — too much empty space — back in, on purpose, to hit a number.

Musical and realistic

A toggle, because the liberty above is the only interesting question here and it should be the reader's to answer rather than mine.

Realistic puts every tower at its true angular position and plays the whole mode across four octaves, fourteen voices. It is closer to what the skyline literally says and correspondingly less kind.

Musical equalises the spacing and forces the sound into a chord: only the first, third, fifth and sixth degrees of the mode, over two and a half octaves, seven voices at a time. Every subset of those notes is consonant, so no bearing in any city can produce a dissonance — not because it is avoided but because the dissonant intervals are not in the instrument.

The first attempt at that used the first, third, fifth and seventh, which on a seven-note mode puts a major seventh one semitone under the octave root. An instrument advertised as incapable of a minor second contained one. Building a ladder out of consonant degrees is not the same as proving the assembled ladder is consonant, and the assembled ladder is now checked: nothing closer than a minor third in musical, nothing closer than a whole tone in realistic.

It cannot be made to hurt

A bank of sine waves driven by a data set can, at some bearing in some city, line up: twenty partials at full tilt in the two-to-four kilohertz band where hearing is sharpest and least forgiving. That is the sound of a smoke alarm, and no amount of tuning the mapping prevents it, because the mapping does not know what the ear finds painful. So it is made structurally impossible instead, and then measured across every city at every bearing in both modes:

The normalisation was wrong the first time in a way worth recording. It flattened the raw levels and then applied the spectral tilt that makes the thing sound like an instrument, which un-did the flattening. A bearing lighting only low partials came out fourteen times louder than one lighting only high ones. Tilt first, normalise second.

Two cities entered by hand

Wikidata has three buildings on record for Austin and fourteen for Nashville. Austin has something over forty above a hundred metres. That gap is not about the cities; it is about who has been entering data, and coverage of mid-size American downtowns is thin in a way that coverage of Manhattan is not. Both are therefore supplemented from published records, the supplement lives in its own file, and the app says how many of a city's towers were entered by hand whenever there are any. Heights are structural; coordinates are block-level rather than surveyed, which matters less than it sounds because the wheel equalises angular spacing anyway — a coordinate decides what order buildings appear in, not the gap between them.

The key

The first version derived a pitch from the city's median building height. That is defensible, and it means nothing — it made Frankfurt and Busan sound different because their towers differ by nine metres, which is not a fact about either city. So the key is now editorial, and every one of them is sourced. They fall into three tiers, marked in the app so a reader can see how much weight to put on any one:

TierCitiesWhat it is
Piece10A widely known piece of music inseparable from the city, in its usual key. New York is B♭ from the opening glissando of Rhapsody in Blue; Warsaw is F minor from Chopin, who lived there; Moscow is E♭ from the 1812.
Anthem6The national anthem in its conventional published key. Weaker — it is about the country, not the city — but checkable rather than invented.
Mode34Where neither exists, the scale system of the city's own tradition. A Gulf city gets maqam Hijaz because Hijaz is what is played there; Tokyo gets the yo scale; Guangzhou gets yu mode, which is what Cantonese opera uses. The root is arbitrary; the mode is not.

Nothing here claims a city is a key. It claims that if you had to pick one, this is the pick with a reason attached — and the reason is on screen whenever you select the city.

How the bars become sound

Ninety-six bars is the right number to look at and the wrong number to play. Four octaves of a five-note scale is twenty pitches, so five or six adjacent bars would land on the same note and become five oscillators detuned by nothing, beating against each other. The bars are mapped onto the distinct pitches of the scale instead, and a pitch takes the loudest bar that feeds it: twenty-odd real partials, ninety-six bars, no unison mud.

Property of the barProperty of the soundWhy
Position across the viewPitch, four octaves of the city's scaleA skyline is not a melody, and there is no reason to make an arbitrary arrangement of towers sound like a mistake.
Apparent heightLoudness of that partial, as height to the power 1.6The exponent makes the peaks stand out in the ear the way they already do in the picture.
Empty horizonSilenceWhich is why turning away from the towers quiets the instrument rather than merely changing it.

Where to stand

A skyline is a thing seen from outside, so the observer has to be put somewhere, and the choice changes the picture completely. The model places them beyond the ninetieth percentile of the tower cluster, on the bearing from which the greatest total angular height falls in front of them — roughly what a photographer does. For the cities whose classic view is across water, Manhattan and Hong Kong and Chicago, it lands close to the classic view without having been told that the water is there.

The first version of this put the observer at a fixed distance from the centroid instead, which in Dubai left them standing two hundred metres from the Burj Khalifa, filling seventy-five degrees of sky. Being outside the middle of a city is not the same as being outside the city.

What went wrong with the data

The obvious query — things over a hundred and twenty metres, with a height and a coordinate — returns a nine-hundred-and-fifty-metre tower in Rotterdam. Rotterdam has no such tower. The height property applies to anything with a height: mountains, waterfalls, bridges, dams, radio masts, and, in the returned set, forty-nine nuclear weapons tests. It also carries a unit, so a value of 950 may be feet.

Both are now handled. Heights come back normalised to metres by the query itself rather than trusted as written, and the class of every entity is checked against a list of things that are actually buildings. The build reports the largest rejected classes each time it runs, so the filter is visible rather than a silent judgement: at the last run, waterfalls, road bridges, mountains and transmitter masts, in that order. The check that this worked is the one that matters — the tallest building in each city now comes out as the building everyone knows it to be.

Limits

Coverage follows the reference database, not reality, so cities well documented in English are better represented than cities that are not. Several Chinese cities with more tall buildings than Chicago appear here with fewer, because fewer of their towers have been entered. The hundred-and-twenty-metre floor is a convention rather than a fact about what reads as skyline, and raising it thins the picture faster in some cities than others.

And the instrument is not a recording. It does not sound like the city. It sounds like the city's shape, which is a different and much smaller claim.
