One in twenty of an open database's tall buildings in 27 cities is not there

Wikidata, the free database kept alongside Wikipedia, is a source for maps, apps and research. Ask it for every building of 55 meters or more in 27 big cities and it returns 1,160 records. By its own records, 29 of those buildings have been demolished or destroyed. Among them is the old 270 Park Avenue in New York, whose demolition it dates to 2019. Another 25 were never finished or are still going up. That is 54 of the 1,160, about one in twenty, returned by any search that does not check. Of the rest, 58 could not be matched to a standing building in OpenStreetMap, the map drawn from the ground and from aerial imagery. Many of those stand, but they are left out rather than risk drawing a tower that is not there. The towers that pass are below as an instrument, for the 22 cities with at least 16 of them, and for Austin and Nashville, which fall short but stay in the list, marked as thin. Turn a wheel of the city's towers, and the towers in front of you play a chord that grows louder the taller they are.

This data does not update.

Interactive

24 cities, played

Drag the wheel, and the chord changes as the towers in front of you change. Tall towers are loud, short ones are soft, and the tallest tower of every city is equally loud. The top of the screen shows the wheel, with each tower at its height and the towers behind you drawn darker. The bottom shows the same towers as bars, which is what you hear. Each tower links to its Wikidata record.

How the towers were checked

Three heights do three jobs. The search asked for everything of 50 meters or more within 30 kilometers of each city's center, so that it missed nothing near the cut. A building must be 55 meters or more to count or to go on a wheel. The table below counts the buildings of 80 meters or more, the usual sense of a high-rise. Each step, in plain words:

  • Not a building. This step removes bridges, statues, monuments and similar items (159). It then removes television towers, radio masts, observation towers, and clock and church towers (53). Wikidata files these under "building", but no one would call them buildings. So the Eiffel Tower, the CN Tower and Tokyo Skytree are not on the wheels.
  • Not in the city. Wikidata must place the tower inside the city. If Wikidata places a tower only in the city's county or state, the tower must be within 3 kilometers of the city's center. So La Défense is not in Paris, Ramat Gan is not in Tel Aviv, and Yokohama is not in Tokyo (190).
  • Gone or unbuilt, by Wikidata's own record. The record has a demolition date, or it records the building as destroyed, proposed, unfinished, under construction, on hold or cancelled (54).
  • Not matched in OpenStreetMap. A tower stays only if OpenStreetMap meets one of two conditions. In the first, OpenStreetMap links one of its buildings to the Wikidata record and does not mark it as under construction. In the second, OpenStreetMap maps a building within 120 meters of the record's position. That building matches the tower's height (or its number of floors at 3.5 meters each) or its name (58).
  • The same building twice. Two records for one building count as one (9).

Three of the 27 cities do not have enough towers left to fill a wheel (7 of at least 80 meters and 16 in all): Miami, Rotterdam and Fort Worth. Austin and Nashville fall short too: Austin has 4 towers of at least 80 meters and 5 in all; Nashville has 12 and 14. They stay in the app's list, marked as thin, and their wheels show only the towers the filters confirm. That is a gap in the database, not in the cities. What is measured and what is invented

Measured. Each tower's height and position come from Wikidata, and whether it stands comes from Wikidata and OpenStreetMap together. The order of the towers around the wheel is their true compass order. The viewpoint is the middle of the city's towers, a point moved toward the tallest. Heights are Wikidata's. OpenStreetMap gives a height for 675 of the towers on the wheels. For 150 of them, that height differs from Wikidata's by more than ten per cent. The wheels use Wikidata's.

Rearranged. The spacing around the wheel. The towers keep their true compass order, clockwise from north, but the spacing between them is even. Seen from the middle, a city's towers cluster: half of them are within an arc of 11 degrees in Dubai and 144 in Warsaw. So a wheel at true angles would be crowded on one side and empty on the other. A transit map takes the same liberty with distance, for the same reason. One result deserves a plain statement: a wheel has no empty horizon, so a turn changes the chord and never silences it.

Invented. The sound, all of it. In no sense does a city have a pitch. The mapping is a choice, and the table below gives it in full. It is deterministic: the same city at the same bearing always makes the same sound.

How the bars become sound

In short: the taller the towers in front of you, the louder the chord. At most seven notes sound at once, and filters process the sound so that it can never become shrill. The details:

Property of the barProperty of the soundWhy
Position across the viewWhich note of the city's chord, lowest on the left; the lowest note is the root an octave downThe view has ninety-six bars, which is too many to play: neighboring bars on almost the same pitch would beat against each other. So the bars share the chord's nine or ten notes, and each note takes the loudest bar that feeds it.
Height, as a share of this city's tallestLoudness of that note, as the share to the power 1.6; and the level of the whole chord, set by its loudest noteThe exponent makes the peaks stand out in the ear as they do in the picture. A turn toward the tallest towers makes the chord swell. A tower half the height of the city's tallest is about ten decibels quieter when you face it. Every city's tallest tower is equally loud: its lower height does not make Brussels quieter than Dubai.
How many bars are litAt most seven notes sound at once, the loudest sevenA bearing that lights every note gives a chord nobody wrote, and density is most of what makes a tone like this harsh.
Which note it isHigher notes are made quieter; every fourth voice is a triangle wave, the rest are sine wavesA choice, so that the chord sounds warm, not buzzy.
EverythingA filter that cuts 12 decibels above 1.8 kHz, another that removes everything above 2.4 kHz, and a limiter, all before the level is setSo that no city at any bearing can pile its notes into a shrill tone.

The keys

Each city's key is a choice with a reason. The reasons are of three kinds, and the app marks them. The first is a piece of music tied to the city, in its usual key. The second is a national anthem, which belongs to the country, not the city. The third is the scale family of the city's musical tradition, with the root chosen.

Which notes of each mode sound is also a choice. Each mode has its own voicing, with the note that gives the mode its character an octave up. There it colors the chord and does not clash with the root. No two notes that sound together are ever closer than three semitones. A line under the bars in the app names the notes that sound at each moment. So you can read the instrument as well as hear it.

Limits

Standing is judged by agreement between two databases that volunteers edit, not by anyone looking. Many of the 58 towers that OpenStreetMap could not confirm do stand, among them Meiji University's Liberty Tower in Tokyo. OpenStreetMap maps them, but without a link, a height or a name that the match could use. A tower not yet built could still pass if Wikidata does not mark it and a building of similar height stands at its position. The located-in test misses towers that Wikidata places nowhere useful. Within 3 kilometers of the center it accepts towers placed only in the city's county or state, and beyond that distance it rejects them. Heights are Wikidata's and some are wrong. It gives the Empire State Building an architectural height of 453 meters, where Wikipedia gives 443.2 meters to the tip and 381 to the roof. On the wheel, that changes how loud one tower sounds next to New York's tallest, not which is tallest. Both databases change daily. The searches behind this page ran on 24 October 2026. Their answers are kept, so the page can be rebuilt from exactly what it was built from.
