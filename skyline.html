Both are a row of vertical bars of varying height. That is a coincidence of drawing conventions rather than of physics, but it is usable: if a skyline can be read as a spectrum, it can be played, and the sound will change as the listener turns. This tool does that for twenty-seven cities, using published building heights and coordinates.

The upper panel is a wheel of the city's towers, up to a hundred and twenty of them, with the far side drawn in a colder colour because it is behind the listener. The lower panel is the same ring as a meter, and the meter is what sounds. Drag to turn, or press Spin.

What is measured and what is invented

Sonification tends to blur the line between what was observed and what was composed, so it is drawn sharply here.

Measured. Each building carries a published structural height and a published coordinate: from Wikidata for twenty-four cities, each tower linked to its record from the app; from the city's published tallest-buildings list (Wikipedia, retrieved 2026) for Austin, Nashville and Fort Worth, where Wikidata had three, fourteen and a handful of buildings on record. The order of the towers around the wheel is their true compass order from the height-weighted centre of the city, and the heights on the wheel are metres.

Chosen. The listener stands at that centre. A tower closer than four hundred metres is held at four hundred, so a building you are standing under does not dwarf the rest; the app writes "under 0.4 km" rather than a number for those. The wheel takes the city's tallest buildings down to fifty-five metres, at most a hundred and twenty of them; the headline count uses eighty. For the three supplemented cities the published list overrules Wikidata above the height the list says it is complete to. Every one of these floors is shown in the app's side panel.

Rearranged. The spacing around the wheel. Buildings hold their true compass order, clockwise from north; the angle between any two of them does not survive. A downtown occupies perhaps forty degrees of a real horizon, and a wheel that reproduced that would be two-thirds empty. A transit map takes the same liberty with distance, for the same reason. One consequence is worth stating plainly: there is no empty horizon on a wheel, so turning changes the chord and never silences it.

Invented. The sound, all of it. There is no sense in which a city has a pitch. The mapping is a choice, it is written out in full below, and it is deterministic: the same city at the same bearing always makes the same sound.

Until 4 September 2026 this section described a different rendering: a true panorama from a viewpoint outside the city, with occlusion and silence off-axis. That rendering was retired before the page went up, but its data was still shipped and its behaviour was still labelled "measured" here. The data is gone from the app and the label with it.

How the bars become sound

Every row of this table is a choice. None of it was measured, and the app says so where the sound is made.

Property of the barProperty of the soundWhy
Position across the viewWhich note of the city's chord: nine to eleven notes over two and a half octaves, lowest on the left, with the root an octave down as a droneNinety-six bars is the right number to look at and the wrong number to play; adjacent bars on one note would be oscillators detuned by nothing, beating. A note takes the loudest bar that feeds it.
Height, as a share of this city's tallestLoudness of that note, as the share to the power 1.6The exponent makes the peaks stand out in the ear the way they do in the picture. Normalising per city means every city's tallest tower is equally loud: Fort Worth is not quieter than New York for being shorter.
How many bars are litAt most seven notes sound at once, the loudest sevenA bearing that lights every note is a chord nobody wrote. Density is most of what makes an additive tone harsh.
Which note it isHigher notes are tilted quieter, by one over the square root of their index; every fourth voice is a triangle wave, the rest are sinesAn instrument loses energy with height; a flat spectrum is a buzz. The triangles give the low voices a little edge.
EverythingA shelf pulling down 1.8 kHz and above, a low-pass at 3.2 kHz, a limiter, and a fixed ceilingSo that no city at any bearing can line up its partials in the band the ear is most sensitive in. The mapping cannot know what hurts; the instrument is built so it cannot produce it.

Which notes of each mode sound is also a choice, and until 4 September 2026 it was the wrong one: the same four scale degrees were taken from every mode and then anything closer than a minor third to its neighbour was dropped, which took the flattened second out of hijaz and left eight modes sounding as five. Dubai in D hijaz was Vienna in D major, note for note. Each mode is now voiced on its own terms, with the tone that makes it itself placed an octave up, where it colours the chord instead of clashing with the root beside it. No two sounding notes are ever closer than a minor third.
