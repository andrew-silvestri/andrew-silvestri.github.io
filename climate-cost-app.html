The true climate cost of a thing

Pick an item, where it was produced, and where it is eaten. The model returns every step, and what each step is made of.

- kg CO2e per kg

What the numbers mean

The spine is the life of the thing. Each bar is a stage, from the field to the bin, and its length is that stage's share of the total. Open one and the branches below show where the emissions inside it came from - and those branches have branches. A tomato needs fertiliser, the fertiliser needs ammonia, the ammonia needs natural gas, and extracting that gas leaks methane. Four levels down, all of it real.

The number in the violet box is an allocation factor. It says what share of a process belongs to this product. A slaughterhouse also makes leather and tallow; a dairy cow also becomes beef; a coffee mill also sells pulp. Charging the whole burden to the one product you care about is a choice, and usually an invisible one. Here it is written on the edge, and it multiplies down the chain, so a process four levels deep may contribute only a third of what it emits.

This is where most disagreement between published footprints actually lives. Two studies of the same cheese can differ twofold without either being wrong, because one split the cow's methane between milk and meat by economic value and the other by protein mass. The number is a judgement. It should be visible.

Three things worth trying

TryWhat happens
Field tomato against heated greenhouse, same route About fivefold. How a thing is grown beats how far it moved.
Any item, switch freight from sea to air Sea freight is nearly free per tonne-kilometre; air is roughly ninety times worse. This is the one case where distance really does decide.
Beef from anywhere, to anywhere Barely moves. Enteric methane and land use happen before anything is shipped, so the route is noise against them.

What this model does not do

It reports greenhouse gases and nothing else. Almonds are usually criticised on water, and this model is silent about water; saying so is better than letting a low carbon number imply a clean bill of health. There is no biodiversity term, no eutrophication, no labour.

Emission factors are representative of a class of production, not measurements of a particular farm. The published spread within a single food is enormous - beef ranges roughly tenfold between the best and worst producers, far more than the gap between many foods - so a point estimate here should be read as an order of magnitude with a plausible shape, not as a measurement.

Expansion stops when a branch falls below a set share of the total, and the model reports how much it dropped so that the omission is visible rather than silent. Land use change is amortised over twenty years by convention; that convention is doing real work in the beef number and a different window would give a different answer.
