The atlas

Seven layers of one model. 7,192 nodes, each on a real coordinate.

Open the atlas full screen

The atlas runs entirely in your browser. Choose a scenario and press Run. The side panel reports how many nodes changed, how many steps the model needed to settle, and the largest single effect. The counts on the tabs show where the change landed in the other layers. Click any node to read its data source, then apply your own change there.

Why a globe, and why a brain

Six of the seven layers are drawn on a sphere rather than a flat map, and the change is not cosmetic. Mercator inflates Greenland to the size of Africa and stretches the high-latitude Russian and Canadian grids into ribbons — which is precisely where a good deal of this model's fuel supply lives. On a globe every node sits at its true angular position, and a point on the far side is hidden by the planet instead of being flung to the edge of a rectangle. The graticule runs every thirty degrees so that rotation reads as rotation.

The seventh layer is not a place, so it has no business on a map. Behaviour is drawn inside a brain, with the six channels at anatomically defensible positions: present bias in the prefrontal cortex, dissonance at the anterior cingulate near the midline, the norm channel at the temporo-parietal junction, status in the ventral striatum, habit in the basal ganglia, loss aversion in the amygdala. The solid itself is generated rather than traced — an ellipsoid given a longitudinal fissure and a few folds, with a cerebellum and a stem attached. It reads as a brain at a glance and it is not anatomy; the application says so on screen. The region names, and which channel sits in which, are the part that is real.
