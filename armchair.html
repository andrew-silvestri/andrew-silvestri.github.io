Right guesses from antiquity waited a median of 2,088 years for confirmation

In March 1610 Galileo reported that a telescope turned on the Milky Way showed it to be a mass of stars, as Democritus had said some two thousand years before. Histories of science collect moments like this one. This page follows 52 such ideas, right and wrong, from the year they were written to the dated event that settled each one, and counts the misses with the hits.

This data does not update.

  1. Followed from the year each was written to the dated event that settled it, right guesses about nature from antiquity waited a median of 2,088 years, mostly for an instrument to be built.
  2. Among ideas from 1400 on, wrong ones were refuted in a median of 34 years, faster than right ones were confirmed (a median of 167).
  3. Of the 52 ideas, five are strong matches to present science and 18 are wrong; the cases are the ones histories cite, so they show waiting times, not how often guesses were right.

Why follow the misses too?

The usual list of anticipations is built backwards. It starts from a modern result, such as atoms, a round Earth or galaxies beyond our own, and searches the old texts for a sentence that resembles it. With enough texts, some sentence always does. The historian Quentin Skinner gave the habit a name in 1969, the mythology of prolepsis1: reading an old author for the significance a later age gives the words, not for what the author meant. A fair count needs a sample that was drawn before the outcome was known, and that keeps the claims nobody now remembers.

No list of cases fully escapes that habit. The 52 cases here are the ideas that histories of science cite, joined by the best-known confident claims that failed, so they can show how long a guess waited but not how often guessing paid off. Each case has a dated passage and one dated decisive event, fixed before the gap between them was computed.

How long did the right answers wait?

Graded against present science, five of the 52 ideas are strong matches, 16 are partial, 13 are loose resemblances and 18 are wrong. The chart above draws each one as a line from the idea to the event.

The ancient hits waited a median of 2,088 years, and in more than half of them the wait ended when an instrument arrived, not an argument. The Milky Way waited for the telescope in 16102. The claim that light takes time to travel, which Aristotle reports from Empedocles and rejects3, waited for Ole Rømer's timings of Jupiter's moon Io in 16764. Atoms waited for Jean Perrin, who counted particles in suspensions under a microscope in 1908 and 19095. Hits from 1400 on waited a median of 167 years. Kant argued in 17556 that the faint elliptical nebulae were whole star systems like our own. Edwin Hubble's distances to two such nebulae, measured from Cepheid stars in them (stars whose pulsing gives away their true brightness), were read out at a meeting in 19257, 170 years later.

Wrong claims from the same period did not last as long: a median of 34 years before a measurement refuted them. Both medians count only ideas that have been settled; an idea still open would lengthen them. In 1835 Auguste Comte8 gave the chemical make-up of the stars as an example of what could never be known; in 1859 Gustav Kirchhoff identified sodium in the Sun's spectrum9. Wilhelm Ostwald argued in 189510 that atoms were a dispensable hypothesis, and conceded in 1908, after Perrin's first results. A claim that names something observable is tested once the instrument exists, and by 1800 the instruments came quickly.

Right in part, wrong in the same passage

The middle grades, partial and loose, hold the most instructive cases. Aristotle worked out that in a void11 all bodies would fall at the same speed, then treated that result as absurd and used it to deny that a void could exist. Edgar Allan Poe, in Eureka (1848), saw that a dark night sky could mean that light from the most distant stars has not yet reached us, then said there was no reason to believe it12. Lord Kelvin made the calculation in 190113 and showed that it accounts for the dark sky. Kant's flat, rotating disc of matter around the young Sun resembles the discs that the Hubble Space Telescope photographed in Orion in 199314, but his mechanism for the rotation breaks the conservation of angular momentum. Even refutations carried errors: Antoine Lavoisier's 1789 table of simple substances15, which ended the four elements, lists light and caloric among them.

When physics answered philosophy

Traffic also ran the other way. Kant held that the axioms16 of Euclid's geometry are known before experience. But the eclipse expeditions of 191917 measured starlight bent by the Sun as general relativity predicted, and the space of that theory is not Euclidean. Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen argued in 193518 that quantum mechanics must be incomplete, on assumptions now called local realism: that no influence travels faster than light, and that measured properties exist before they are measured. Experiments in the following decades favoured quantum mechanics, and in 2015 a test in Delft19 closed the main loopholes at once, 80 years after the paper. A philosophical thesis became a measurement, and the measurement went against it.

None of these thinkers could know which of their ideas would survive. The ones that did were real achievements, and for most of history they waited on instruments nobody had yet built.

Method

Cases. Each case has the year of the passage and one named event, chosen before the gap was computed: a publication or an observation, not textbook acceptance. The gap is the event year minus the idea year. When the idea is BCE and the event is AD, subtract one more, because there is no year 0. The build recomputes every gap and stops if one differs from the case file. "Ancient" means an idea before AD 500; "from 1400 on" means 1400 and after. Medians for hits use strong and partial cases; medians for misses use wrong cases.

Grades. Strong: the core claim matches present science and could have been wrong. Partial: right in one key respect but wrong in mechanism or detail. Loose: a resemblance in words only. Wrong. The grades were assigned while building this page, with an AI language model, and checked against the sources listed for each case. They are judgements, and the case file gives the reason for each.

Limits

"Present science" moves: an idea graded wrong today could gain partial credit later, and the reverse. The 52 cases are not a sample: they are the cases people cite, plus the best-known misses, and they show gaps, not rates. Some passage references in the case files rest on secondary sources. The notes mark which ones.
