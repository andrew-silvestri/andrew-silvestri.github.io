What a meter's error is worth

An orifice gas meter carries a standard uncertainty of about a quarter of a per cent. Across a system of them, what that is worth depends less on how good each meter is than on which of their errors they share, and that is the one thing no open source settles.

An orifice meter measures gas by the strength of a pressure drop. The plate is a disc with a hole in it, clamped across a pipe; the gas speeds up through the hole, its pressure falls, and the fall says how fast it is going. Every term in that inference carries an error: the coefficient that turns a pressure drop into a flow, the transmitters that read the pressures and the temperature, the bore of the hole, the inside of the pipe, the analysis of the gas and the equation that says how far it is from ideal. Add them up for one well-kept meter and the answer is a fraction of a per cent.

A pipeline system is many such meters. Gas comes in through some and goes out through others, and the difference between the two totals is the system's imbalance. Part of any imbalance is real loss. Part of it is measurement, and this page asks how big that part is and what it costs. It answers with a model of a synthetic system in which no gas is lost at all, so that every unit of imbalance it finds is a measurement error.

What it finds

For one meter, the discharge coefficient is the whole story. On a 102.3 mm run at a beta ratio of 0.50 the meter's flow carries a standard uncertainty of 0.27% over one calibration interval, and the discharge coefficient on its own gives 0.25% (the inputs combine as the square root of the sum of their squares, so the parts do not add up to the whole). The next largest input, the bore diameter, contributes 0.062%; differential pressure 0.044%, temperature 0.054%. A single meter's uncertainty lives in the equation, not in the instruments.

One receipt meter of the default system, each input alone and then all together, as standard uncertainty (one standard deviation) of its flow. Doubled, the combined figure is 0.54%, beside 0.55% at 95% confidence for the example station in a published uncertainty handbook1; that was computed for another meter and is a scale, not a check.

In the meters modelled here that coefficient is not measured. It comes from an equation, Reader-Harris/Gallagher, fitted to laboratory data2 and written into ISO 5167-23, and a later calculation that took in the data behind that fit and the manufacturing variation the standard permits made an insignificant difference to its stated uncertainty. Whether one meter's error in it is the same as another's is the question that decides everything below, and no public source settles it for meters at different sites. A handbook on metering-station uncertainty4 treats the coefficient as uncorrelated between two runs side by side, because their inlet pipework is never the same; the equation's own bias, which every meter that uses it shares, points the other way. So the model runs three cases and does not choose between them.

The default system has 12 receipt meters on 102.3 mm runs feeding 3 delivery meters on larger ones, 101 million cubic feet a day in all. Its measured imbalance, receipts minus deliveries as a share of throughput, has a standard uncertainty of:

The middle row is the counter-intuitive one. An error every meter shares is the worst kind for the total a single meter or a group of them reports, because nothing averages it away. It is the best kind for a balance, because it inflates receipts and deliveries by the same factor and the difference does not move. Here receipts and deliveries run at beta ratios of 0.50 and 0.55, which this model puts in one class sharing the equation's error (an assumption), and the imbalance left is 0.055%, all of it from the rest of the budget. Make the ends' errors shared within each end but independent between them, and the same coefficient stops cancelling and roughly doubles in effect: in the bottom row it alone accounts for 0.35%, against 0.17% when every meter's error is its own.

The default system's measured imbalance in energy, as a share of throughput, over one year in which every transmitter is recalibrated four times. Each curve is 200,000 simulated years; the true imbalance is zero in all of them. The axis stops at plus and minus 1%, which leaves out 0.5% of the ends-apart years.

In money, one standard uncertainty of imbalance on this system is between $65,000 and $420,000 a year at $3.10 per million British thermal units, the median Henry Hub spot price5 from 2025-10 to 2026-09. The mean over the same months is $3.59, pulled up by one month at $7.72, which is why the median is used.

What averages away

The intuition that more meters means a better total is right for errors that are each meter's own and wrong for errors they share. With the discharge coefficient independent, the total of the receipt meters' readings goes from 0.26% uncertain with one meter, over a year of four calibrations, to 0.030% with a hundred. With it shared, a hundred meters are barely better than one: 0.26% to 0.25%. The imbalance follows neither rule on its own, because it is a difference, and whether a shared error survives a difference depends on whether both sides share it.

N receipt meters into one delivery meter sized to take them, in the same three cases. Panel A is what a seller's total is good to; panel B is what the balance between seller and buyer is good to.

For scale, the U.S. Energy Information Administration's national figure for natural gas "losses and unaccounted for6" in 2024 was 1.6% of consumption. That number is not a measurement error and should not be read as one. Of it, 0.5 points are losses the companies know of, from leaks, damage and blowdown; the other 1.1 are "unaccounted for", the residual between what companies report as supply and what they report as disposition, which the agency's note says may come from quantities lost or from "differences in company accounting systems in terms of scope and definition." Distribution utilities report a percentage of their own to the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration7 each year; across 1,368 natural gas reports for 2025 the median was 1.00%, and 234 of them were negative, more gas out than in.

Method

Each meter's mass flow follows ISO 5167-2: the Reader-Harris/Gallagher discharge coefficient8, iterated with the Reynolds number it depends on, and the standard's expansibility factor. The model runs its own transcription of the equation; the open implementation used9 to check it is the Python library fluids, which it matches to within 2×10−16. Compressibility and the isentropic exponent come from NIST's reference implementation of AGA Report No. 810, the GERG-2008 equation of state, compiled from its published source and checked against NIST's own test points. The heating value comes from the same code's GROSS routine, which NIST marks preliminary, as the ideal gross value at 25 °C rather than 60 °F: about 0.05% in level, which cancels in a relative imbalance. Volume is reported at 14.73 psia and 60 °F, and energy from the gas's ideal gross heating value, so a composition error moves the molar mass, the compressibility and the heating value together.

Every input's error is drawn in the unit its source states it in: transmitter errors as a fraction of span11, which becomes a larger fraction of reading the lower a meter runs in its range; temperature in kelvin12, for the sensor, its transmitter13 and its installation14; the compressibility equation's error in proportion to how far the gas is from ideal15 at each meter's pressure (that scaling is an assumption of this model). Each error has a shared fraction between zero and one, the correlation between any two meters' errors in that input. By default the transmitters are independent from meter to meter, as the handbook classifies them for runs at one station; the diameters and the gas analysis, which it classes as correlated within a station, are also independent here, because these meters are taken to sit at different sites (an assumption the simulator lets a reader change). The compressibility equation is shared by construction, since every meter uses the same one. Transmitter errors and the gas analysis are redrawn at each of four calibrations a year; the plate, the pipe, the coefficient and the equation keep theirs for the year. Each static pressure transmitter's span is taken as 1.5 times its meter's upstream pressure. In the model the true gas is one composition and balances exactly in moles.

Uncertainty is propagated by Monte Carlo after JCGM 101:200816, the supplement to the international guide on expressing measurement uncertainty, with its adaptive stopping rule: batches of 10,000 trials until every reported figure has stabilised to the precision it is printed with. A test suite checks the model against cases with known answers: zero input uncertainty gives zero imbalance, independent errors average as one over the square root of the number of meters, and each shared error cancels or survives exactly as the algebra says. Each check has been run against a deliberately broken model and seen to fail.

Limits

This is an explainer. It gives no engineering advice and states no uncertainty for any real installation. The system is synthetic, sized and set by assumption. The discharge coefficient's uncertainty is the ISO 5167-2 figure as quoted by a manufacturer's technical note, because the standard's text is not openly published; the pipe diameter's 0.25% is an assumption; the bore's 0.05% is the standard's roundness limit17 used as a bound; and the gas analysis uncertainties come from one published example budget for one chromatograph18. Scatter about each transmitter's offset within a calibration interval is taken to average out; the uncertainty of the expansibility factor is left out because its formula is not openly published; and nothing here models a damaged plate, a liquid-laden stream, a pulsating flow or any of the faults that move a real meter further than its stated uncertainty.

The code

model.py holds the meter equation, the error model and the Monte Carlo; build_meter.py runs every case on this page and writes the payload; fig_meter.py draws the three figures; test_meter.py holds the known-answer tests and their deliberate breaks, and checks/ the checks of fluids and of the compiled NIST code against their published answers. aga8/ is NIST's source, a U.S. government work not subject to copyright in the United States, which build_aga8.py compiles; engine.js is the browser port the simulator runs, held to the Python by a parity test.
