The plastics feedstock that U.S. gas users burned as fuel

For several years, the United States burned a raw material for plastic as fuel. In 2014, up to 27 percent of the ethane in U.S. gas went that way, because too few plastics plants and export ships could take it. Ethane left in the gas does not become plastic: it travels with the rest of the gas to its users, is burned in their furnaces, stoves and power plants, and makes the gas hotter. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) found that in July 2014 the gas delivered in West Virginia carried 6 percent more heat than the national average1, and in North Dakota 5 percent more, which it said may indicate ethane left in. It also found that "stoves and other gas-fueled appliances typically require natural gas to be within a certain range of Btu content" (Btu, British thermal units, measure heat). The choice is made at gas processing plants, which can take the ethane out of the gas and sell it as a liquid, mostly to be made into ethylene for plastics, or leave it in. EIA calls leaving it in ethane rejection. But how much was left in? In May 2014 EIA reported estimates of 200,000 to 400,000 barrels a day, without saying whose they were. Taken with the year's average recovery, a base this page chose, they come to about 15 to 27 percent of the ethane in the gas the plants processed. The headline share is an estimate twice over. Plants have taken more of the ethane out since, as plastics plants and export ships took more, but EIA wrote in 2025 that it can still be left in when its price comes close to that of gas.

Archived page. This page is a finished record, built from the monthly figures of the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) through July 2026 and its articles of 2013 to 2025. It will not be updated.

  1. When plants leave ethane in the gas, ethane falls behind the other four liquids that the plants produce (propane, two butanes and natural gasoline). Ethane's share of the five liquids fell from 41.9 percent in 2010 to 33.8 percent in 2015, while propane output grew faster than ethane output.
  2. The share was 42.0 percent over the twelve months to July 2026, about the same as in 2010, after new plastics plants and the first ethane export ships began to take more of it.
  3. Since 2015, ethane production has grown 2.9 times and the other plant liquids 2.0 times. For 2024, a record year, EIA gave two causes: more recovery as gas production increased, and continued growth in demand from petrochemical plants at home and abroad.

How can a count of barrels show what was left in?

Gas plants produce five liquids (ethane, propane, two butanes and natural gasoline), and when they process more gas they produce more of all five, so a rise in the volume of ethane says little by itself. Its share of the five says more, because EIA identifies ethane as different: "Unlike heavier natural gas plant liquids—such as propane, butanes, and natural gasoline—significant amounts of ethane can be left in natural gas transported on pipelines to natural gas customers". When plants leave ethane in the gas, its share falls. EIA used the same measure in2 2016: it reported that the share decreased from 42 percent in 2010 to 34 percent in 2015.

The chart shows ethane's share month by month, which moves by a few points within each year. Below it, ethane and propane production run on scales of their own.

2009 to 2012: before the fall. Ethane was 41.9 percent of the liquids that U.S. gas plants produced in 2010, and still 40.5 percent in 2012.

2013 to 2015: ethane fell behind. From 2012 to 2015, propane production rose from 712 to 1,144 thousand barrels a day, while ethane production rose only from 975 to 1,130, and ethane's share fell to 33.8 percent in 2015.

2016 to 2019: more of it recovered. The first ship exports left in March 2016 and new petrochemical plants started operation. Ethane production rose 20 percent in 2018 alone, and its share rose again to 39.2 percent.

Since 2020: back near the 2010 share. Over the twelve months to July 2026, plants produced 3.27 million barrels a day of ethane, 42.0 percent of the liquids, against 41.9 percent in 2010.

Data: EIA's monthly field production of natural gas plant liquids, January 2009 to July 2026, as fetched on October 25, 2026. The latest months are preliminary, and EIA revises them. The share is ethane over EIA's total for the five liquids3.

The price, in EIA's words

According to EIA, ethane sold for about the price of natural gas from late 2012 to mid-2017, measured by the heat each carries, and at a premium since. This page found no ethane price series that is free to republish4, so it computes no price signal of its own and gives EIA's words instead, at the dates EIA wrote them. The one series that could show when recovery paid is the one series this page could not find free to reprint.

Prices are for ethane at Mont Belvieu, Texas, in EIA's words "the main pricing hub for NGPLs". MMBtu is a million British thermal units, a measure of heat; EIA gives ethane's approximate heat content11 as 2.783 MMBtu a barrel, the value that lets a price per gallon be set beside a price per MMBtu.

Price was not the only factor. In 2016, EIA described what had changed12: "Beginning in 2012, the availability of relatively inexpensive ethane encouraged a wave of investments in ethane-consuming petrochemical plants and export facilities". It dated the first ship exports of ethane, from a terminal at Marcus Hook, Pennsylvania, to March 9, 2016. In 2019 it wrote that "Several new petrochemical crackers were commissioned in the United States in 201813", and that ethane is recovered "if ethane's value is sufficient to cover the additional costs to produce and distribute the ethane to markets". Of the record year 2024, it wrote: "Increasing ethane recovery associated with natural gas production and continued growth in the domestic and global petrochemical sectors drove these increases".

Method

Volumes. The data is EIA's monthly field production of natural gas plant liquids by product for the United States, in thousand barrels a day: ethane, propane, normal butane, isobutane and natural gasoline. EIA also gives their total, and the build checks each month that the total equals the sum of the five. A year's share is the mean of its monthly shares. The share over the latest twelve months (August 2025 to July 2026) is the ethane of those months divided by their total, and growth since 2015 compares the same twelve months with the 2015 average. EIA has also compared ethane with the gas produced: in 2019 it wrote that "Dry natural gas production increased much more slowly over this time period, indicating that natural gas processors were extracting more, and consequently, rejecting less ethane". This page uses the share because it is national and monthly.

The 2014 estimate. On May 8, 2014, EIA wrote: "Estimates of ethane rejection volumes range between 200 and 400 Mbbl/d" (thousand barrels a day). It did not say whose estimates they were. In 2014 plants recovered 1,091 thousand barrels a day on average, so the range is 15.5 to 26.8 percent of the ethane recovered and left in together. Two choices sit under that percent: a range EIA reported without a source, and a base (the year's average recovery) that this page chose to set it against. It is a reasonable estimate, not a measurement.

Checked against EIA. The build reproduces figures that EIA printed from the same series, among them ethane's share of 42 percent in 2010 and 34 percent in 2015, and 1.71 million barrels a day in 2018. The code lists all of them. EIA statements that the series does not reproduce are left off the page.

Limits

This page is a record of what EIA published, not a model. With no price series, it cannot show month by month when the recovery of ethane paid; it can show the volumes and repeat what EIA wrote. The share also moves with the gas itself, which runs wetter or drier from one basin to the next, as well as with the ethane that plants leave in. So it shows the direction of rejection but not its amount, and it cannot show how much ethane is still left in the gas today. In 2025, EIA wrote that "when ethane prices and natural gas prices are closer, more ethane can be left in the natural gas stream and sold for its heat value". The totals are national, and EIA found rejection "particularly evident in 2013 in the Marcellus Shale play in Pennsylvania, West Virginia, and Ohio, as well as in the Bakken formation in North Dakota". A national share does not show clearly where it occurred.
