The plastics feedstock that U.S. gas users burned as fuel

In 2014, up to 27 per cent of the ethane in U.S. gas, a raw material for plastic, was burned as fuel instead, because too few plastics plants and export ships could take it. Ethane left in the gas does not become plastic: it goes to gas users with the rest of the gas, is burned in their furnaces, stoves and power plants, and makes the gas hotter. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) found that in July 2014 the gas delivered in West Virginia carried 6 per cent more heat than the national average1, and in North Dakota 5 per cent more, which it said may indicate ethane left in. It also found that "stoves and other gas-fueled appliances typically require natural gas to be within a certain range of Btu content" (Btu, British thermal units, measure heat). The choice is made at gas processing plants, which can take the ethane out of the gas and sell it as a liquid, mostly to be made into ethylene for plastics, or leave it in. EIA calls leaving it in ethane rejection. In May 2014 EIA reported estimates of 200,000 to 400,000 barrels a day, about 15 to 27 per cent of the ethane in the gas the plants processed. Plants have taken more of it out since, as plastics plants and export ships took more, but EIA wrote in 2025 that ethane can still be left in when its price comes close to that of gas.

  1. When plants leave ethane in the gas, ethane falls behind the other four liquids that the plants produce: propane, two butanes and natural gasoline. Ethane's share of the five liquids fell from 41.9 per cent in 2010 to 33.8 per cent in 2015. At the same time, propane output grew faster than ethane output.
  2. The share was 42.0 per cent over the twelve months to July 2026, about the same as in 2010. This came after new plastics plants and the first ethane export ships began to take more of it.
  3. Since 2015, ethane production has grown 2.9 times and the other plant liquids 2.0 times. For 2024, a record year, EIA gave two causes. The causes were more recovery as gas production increased, and continued growth in demand from petrochemical plants at home and abroad.

Measuring what was left in

Gas plants produce five liquids: ethane, propane, two butanes and natural gasoline. When plants process more gas, they produce more of all five liquids. Thus an increase in the volume of ethane does not show much by itself. Ethane's share of the five liquids shows more, because EIA identifies ethane as different: "Unlike heavier natural gas plant liquids—such as propane, butanes, and natural gasoline—significant amounts of ethane can be left in natural gas transported on pipelines to natural gas customers". When plants leave ethane in the gas, its share decreases. EIA used the same measure in2 2016: it reported that the share decreased from 42 per cent in 2010 to 34 per cent in 2015.

The chart shows ethane's share month by month. The share changes by a few points within each year. Below the share, the chart shows ethane and propane production, each on its own scale.

2009 to 2012: before the fall. Ethane was 41.9 per cent of the liquids that U.S. gas plants produced in 2010, and 40.5 per cent in 2012.

2013 to 2015: ethane fell behind. From 2012 to 2015, propane production increased from 712 to 1,144 thousand barrels a day. Ethane production increased only from 975 to 1,130. Ethane's share decreased to 33.8 per cent in 2015.

2016 to 2019: more of it recovered. The first ship exports left in March 2016, and new petrochemical plants started operation. Ethane production increased 20 per cent in 2018 alone, and its share increased again to 39.2 per cent.

Since 2020: back near the 2010 share. Over the twelve months to July 2026, plants produced 3.27 million barrels a day of ethane. This was 42.0 per cent of the liquids, compared with 41.9 per cent in 2010.

Data: EIA's monthly field production of natural gas plant liquids, January 2009 to July 2026, as fetched on October 25, 2026. The latest months are preliminary, and EIA revises them. The share is ethane over EIA's total for the five liquids3.

The price, in EIA's words

According to EIA, ethane sold for about the price of natural gas from late 2012 to mid-2017, measured by the heat each carries. Since then, it has sold at a premium. This page found no ethane price series that is free to republish4. Thus it does not compute a price signal of its own; it gives EIA's words, at the dates EIA wrote them.

Prices are for ethane at Mont Belvieu, Texas, in EIA's words "the main pricing hub for NGPLs". MMBtu is a million British thermal units, a measure of heat. EIA gives ethane's approximate heat content11 as 2.783 MMBtu a barrel. With this value, a price per gallon and a price per MMBtu can be compared.

Price was not the only factor. In 2016, EIA described what had changed12: "Beginning in 2012, the availability of relatively inexpensive ethane encouraged a wave of investments in ethane-consuming petrochemical plants and export facilities". EIA dated the first ship exports of ethane, from a terminal at Marcus Hook, Pennsylvania, to March 9, 2016. In 2019 it wrote that "Several new petrochemical crackers were commissioned in the United States in 201813", and that ethane is recovered "if ethane's value is sufficient to cover the additional costs to produce and distribute the ethane to markets". Of the record year 2024, it wrote: "Increasing ethane recovery associated with natural gas production and continued growth in the domestic and global petrochemical sectors drove these increases".

Method

Volumes. The data is EIA's monthly field production of natural gas plant liquids by product, for the United States, in thousand barrels a day. The products are ethane, propane, normal butane, isobutane and natural gasoline. EIA also gives their total, and the build checks that the total equals the sum of the five each month. A year's share is the mean of its monthly shares. The share over the latest twelve months, August 2025 to July 2026, is the ethane of those months divided by their total. Growth since 2015 compares the latest twelve months with the 2015 average. EIA also compared ethane with the gas produced. In 2019 it wrote that "Dry natural gas production increased much more slowly over this time period, indicating that natural gas processors were extracting more, and consequently, rejecting less ethane". This page uses the share because it is national and monthly.

The 2014 estimate. On May 8, 2014, EIA wrote: "Estimates of ethane rejection volumes range between 200 and 400 Mbbl/d" (thousand barrels a day). EIA did not say whose estimates they were. In 2014, plants recovered 1,091 thousand barrels a day on average. Thus the range is 15.5 to 26.8 per cent of the ethane recovered and left in together.

Checked against EIA. The build reproduces figures that EIA printed from the same series. Among them are ethane's share, 42 per cent in 2010 and 34 per cent in 2015, and 1.71 million barrels a day in 2018. The code lists all of them. This page does not include EIA statements that the series does not reproduce.

Limits

This page is a record of what EIA published, not a model. It has no price series, so it cannot show month by month when the recovery of ethane paid. It can only show the volumes and repeat what EIA wrote. The share changes with the gas itself, which is wetter or drier from one basin to the next. It also changes with the quantity of ethane that plants leave in. Thus the share shows the direction of rejection, not its amount, and it cannot show how much ethane is still left in the gas today. In 2025, EIA wrote that "when ethane prices and natural gas prices are closer, more ethane can be left in the natural gas stream and sold for its heat value". The totals are national: EIA found rejection "particularly evident in 2013 in the Marcellus Shale play in Pennsylvania, West Virginia, and Ohio, as well as in the Bakken formation in North Dakota", and a national share does not show clearly where it occurred.
