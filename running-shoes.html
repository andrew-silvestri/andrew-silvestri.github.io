What a running shoe is made of

Six or seven polymers, bonded to each other, in a product with a working life measured in months. The recycling problem is in the construction, not the disposal.

A running shoe is a good object. It is also, materially, close to a worst case: a laminate of chemically dissimilar plastics, glued and welded together, worn out in a few hundred miles, and produced in the billions. The usual response — a take-back bin at the shop door — addresses the last step of a problem that was decided at the design stage. This page takes three shoes apart on paper and says which part is which polymer, then follows each one to the end.

The short version

Nothing here shares a recycling stream

The upper is polyester. The overlays and heel counter are TPU. The midsole is EVA, PEBA, expanded TPU, or two of them stacked. The outsole is vulcanised rubber. A racing shoe adds carbon fibre. These are bonded, so they arrive at end of life as one object made of everything.

The stack, from the ground up

Outsole. Vulcanised rubber, usually carbon rubber where abrasion matters and blown rubber where weight does. Vulcanisation is a thermoset cure: the rubber is cross-linked and cannot be melted and reformed, which rules out the recycling route that works for the rest of the shoe.

Midsole. The part that has changed most and the reason shoes are now multi-material problems rather than one material problem. EVA — ethylene-vinyl acetate — is the traditional foam: cheap, light, and it packs out. Expanded TPU is a bead foam with far better rebound, which is what the Adidas Boost generation demonstrated. PEBA, a polyether block amide, foamed supercritically, is the current racing material: lighter again, higher energy return, and stable across temperatures. Most performance shoes now stack two of these, often with a carbon-fibre plate laminated between them.

Upper. Engineered polyester mesh, frequently containing recycled PET, with thermoplastic polyurethane overlays for structure: a heel counter to hold the rearfoot, eyestays to spread lace tension, a toe bumper. These are heat-welded or glued to the mesh rather than stitched, because welding is lighter, faster, and produces no seam to irritate the foot.

Everything between. Adhesive. A midsole is glued to an outsole; an upper is lasted and glued to a midsole; overlays are bonded to mesh. The MIT life-cycle assessment of a pair of running shoes counted 65 discrete parts and more than 360 processing steps. Each of those bonds is a place where two incompatible polymers are permanently joined.

Why the recycling fails

Mechanical recycling of plastics requires a clean, single-polymer feed. Mixed polymers do not melt into a usable material; they melt into something with the properties of the worst constituent and the price of none of them. A shoe cannot be shredded into a feedstock because the shreds are not one material, and it cannot be economically disassembled into single materials because the joints were designed to be permanent.

So what take-back schemes actually do is slice the shoe into three coarse streams — outsole rubber, midsole foam, textile upper — grind each separately, and sell the granulate into applications tolerant of imperfect input: running track surfaces, playground matting, court underlay. That separation is real, and it is coarse: it sorts a shoe into three mixtures rather than into polymers, and the midsole stream alone may hold EVA, PEBA and expanded TPU. This is downcycling, and it is worth being precise about what that means. The crumb cannot become a shoe, and the surface it becomes will not be recycled again — it is a delay on the way to landfill, not a loop. It also has a genuine benefit, which is that it displaces virgin material in those products. Both things are true.

One number is worth holding onto: the MIT assessment put a typical pair at roughly 13.6 kg of CO2-equivalent, and found that more than two thirds of that can fall in manufacturing rather than in raw materials. That is the strongest argument against treating this as a materials-sourcing problem. Recycled polyester in the upper improves the smaller share. Making shoes last longer, or making fewer of them, addresses the larger one.

What this page is not

It is a synthesis of published materials science and one widely cited life-cycle assessment, not a teardown of specific commercial models. The three shoes in the figure are archetypes — a daily trainer, a plated racer, a trail shoe — drawn to the proportions those categories actually use, not renderings of any particular product. Where a construction detail varies by manufacturer, the figure shows the common case.
