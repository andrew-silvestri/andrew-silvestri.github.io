Critically endangered birds draw about 5 times the research, once the paperwork is set aside

For any bird, most of what a literature search turns up is not research. Search the open research index OpenAlex for a bird's scientific name and, across the 9,113 species on this page, only 31% of what comes back for 2015 to 2024 is research. 59% are datasets, nearly all of them GBIF occurrence downloads, records created automatically each time someone downloads sightings from a biodiversity database; 9% are Red List assessments and Birds of the World encyclopedia entries. Count research alone and compare like with like, birds of the same family, size, range, time since they were named and rated attractiveness. A typical bird of Least Concern, the Red List's lowest-risk category, had 2.0 research works in those ten years and a typical Critically Endangered one 10.1, about 5 times as many. 30% of the birds had no research work at all. Being endangered goes with being studied. Yet none of the six published studies of research effort read in full for this page found that link (three found no link, two the opposite). Which way the arrow runs is not settled here, because a bird cannot be listed as endangered without evidence, and evidence is research.

What a search finds

Three kinds of record name a bird but are not research about it. GBIF, the Global Biodiversity Information Facility, gives each download of its occurrence data a citable identifier and lists the species in its description. Each time a researcher, a student or a consultant downloads the sightings for a region, each bird in them gets a record. These records are 59% of all the records counted here. The Red List publishes each species assessment under its own identifier, and each reassessment gets a new one. The Cornell Lab's Birds of the World does the same for its encyclopedia accounts, and together these two are 9%. Research is 31%: journal articles, reviews, preprints, theses, books and reports. This page uses that count; the remaining 1.4% is peer-review reports, errata and other items.

For the typical bird, threatened species have more records of every kind. With the same terms held equal, a Critically Endangered bird has 2.7 times the GBIF downloads of a Least Concern bird. It has 1.5 times the Red List and Birds of the World entries, because a listing writes some of its own record as reassessments accumulate. But the gap does not come from the paperwork. With every record counted, the Critically Endangered bird has 2.7 times the total of a Least Concern bird; counting only research, 5.0 times. The gap increases when the paperwork is removed. The downloads are most of each bird's count, and they increase less steeply with threat (2.7 times) than research does.

Averages tell a different story, and the reason matters. A Critically Endangered bird has an average of 13 research works and a Least Concern bird 11, almost no difference. About 140 well-known Least Concern birds have a hundred research works or more each, and they increase the Least Concern average. Examples are the red junglefowl (the chicken's wild ancestor), the rock dove, the mallard and the house sparrow, which have hundreds or thousands each. Threat and research go together for the typical bird, the obscure majority.

Threat and research

Research works per bird species by Red List category: as counted, adjusted, and adjusted with public attention also held fixed. "Typical" is the back-transformed mean of log(1 + research works). For counts this skewed, it is closer to a median than to a mean. The adjusted series show the count that each bird would have in each category in turn, with all its other properties unchanged. Whiskers are 95% intervals from the resampling of whole bird families.

As counted, a Least Concern bird has 2.3 research works and a Critically Endangered bird 5.1. Adjusted, the figures are 2.0 and 10.1, with a 95% interval of 7.4 to 13.3 for the Critically Endangered species. The adjusted values increase with each step: Near Threatened 3.1, Vulnerable 4.2, Endangered 6.0. (As counted, Near Threatened is just below Least Concern.) The adjustment makes the gap wider, not smaller. Threatened birds tend to have narrow ranges and recent descriptions, and such birds are hard to study. The adjustment holds these terms equal and thus removes a handicap.

The size of the multiple depends on what is being compared, and the page gives each. For the typical bird it is 5.0 times (95% interval 3.7 to 6.8, from the resampling of whole families). For journal articles and reviews alone it is about the same, 5.0 times. For the average count, the gap is smaller and much less certain. A count model compares averages, so the few hundred birds with large literatures dominate it. That model gives 2.3 times, with an interval from 1.0 to 5.1: only just distinguishable from no difference. In that model, Vulnerable birds are below Near Threatened ones. Thus the finding is about the typical bird, the obscure majority. A critically endangered one of those has several times the research of a comparable Least Concern one. The famous few dominate the averages whatever their status.

Some of the gap travels with public attention. When English Wikipedia views are also held equal, the multiple changes from 5.0 to 3.0. One snapshot cannot show which causes which. A bird with more study possibly gets a better article and more readers; the reverse is equally possible.

What else goes with study

What the fit of the full model depends on: the decrease in R² when each term is removed and the model is fitted again. Whiskers are 95% bootstrap intervals over families. The full model adds Wikipedia views to the adjusted model above; R² increases from 0.48 to 0.58.

The number of Wikipedia views of a bird gives the largest part of the fit (0.101). The family of the bird gives nearly as much (0.083): some families are studied as model organisms, game or pests. Red List category is fifth of seven (0.013), behind years since description and range size. Rated attractiveness, scored from photographs by several thousand people, adds 0.000. Beauty, the quality that this page was made to test, explains almost nothing when the other terms are known. Its coefficient is indistinguishable from zero (p 0.57).

The literature disagrees

Ten species-level models of research effort across mammals, birds, reptiles, turtles and plants have been published since 2014. Of these, eight have a threat-status term in a model. Of the 6 whose full text could be read for this page, 3 find threat status null and 2 find it negative. The remaining one finds that an assessment of any kind, not a threatened status, goes with more study. One of the negative results is the one other study of all birds, from 2014, a comparison with nothing else held equal. Those studies counted papers in Web of Science, Zoological Record or Scopus, mostly on a single class. This page uses a different index, a later window and one class, and any of the three could produce a different sign. A single result against a literature is more likely to be wrong than the literature. Thus this page reports what one instrument measured on one class over one decade.

"Assessed, positive" means that the paper found more study for species on the Red List than for unassessed species. The category has no further effect. The authors note the circularity: an assessment needs data before it can exist. "Unadjusted" marks a comparison with nothing else held equal. For British birds, the international Red List category was null. But the national action-plan listing, with money and a monitoring duty behind it, predicted more study. Rows read from the abstract alone give the result of the abstract; unread rows stay unread, and no result is guessed.

Across whole classes the picture is the familiar one. Amphibians are 41% threatened, the highest best estimate that the Red List gives for any vertebrate class. They hold 25% of the threatened vertebrate species but get 4% of the research works that name their class. That is 6 per threatened species, against 89 for birds.

Method

Birds are the only class with a free, species-level source for each term. AVONET gives body mass11, range size and family for 11,009 species. The iratebirds project12 gives a rated attractiveness score for 9,386 of them. The GBIF backbone gives the year13 of description of each species. For 23 species with no GBIF year, the year is from the Catalogue of Life. English Wikipedia gives a public-attention measure14 for the 10,645 species with an article. OpenAlex gives the number of papers15 and other works of 2015 to 2024 whose title or abstract names the species, split by record type. Research means these types: article, review, preprint, letter, book, book chapter, dissertation, conference paper and report. This list was fixed before any model was fitted on the split. The Red List gives the category. The joined data keeps only species with every term and a category that ranks risk: 9,113 birds.

The response is log(1 + research works). The adjusted model is ordinary least squares with Least Concern as the reference category. Its terms are a fixed effect for each family, log body mass and log range size. It also has years since description to 2024, rated attractiveness and Red List category. The full model adds log(1 + mean monthly Wikipedia views). Standard errors are clustered by family. Category means are predictions, not coefficients: each species is predicted under each category with its other terms unchanged, and the predictions are averaged and back-transformed. The count model is a Poisson regression on the same terms. Intervals on the means are percentile bootstrap over families, 300 resamples, seed 20260905. Family fixed effects are a substitute for a phylogeny, which is assumed; the published bird model used a tree16.

The Red List column is from the Red List API, version 2026-1. It was matched by scientific name to the AVONET names, and 10,669 of 11,009 species matched. Species with no English Wikipedia article get zero views, so the obscure species stay in the data. 17 other species are not in the model because they have no category that the model can use. For most, the name has no match in the Red List; a few are Data Deficient, Extinct or Extinct in the Wild. The species query uses only the OpenAlex title-and-abstract filter, because its short search parameter changed meaning in February 202617. That parameter now means the full text, which returned 3.4 times as many works for the lion. A test asserts the query that the fetcher builds.

Limits

Association, not cause, and here that is not a formality. A bird cannot be listed Critically Endangered without evidence. The higher categories need population, trend and range data, which is research; Least Concern needs much less. Thus the arrow possibly runs from research to listing, and the gradient on this page is what rising evidence requirements would produce on their own. The category that means nobody has enough data points the same way. The 34 Data Deficient birds have a median of 0 research works, against 1 for Least Concern. This comparison uses a wider frame of 10,669 birds with a category and a count. Those Data Deficient birds are too few for a coefficient, so the page gives none.

Record types are OpenAlex's, not this page's. OpenAlex counts a report or a thesis as research, and a short note the same as a monograph. The count misses a paper that names a bird only by its common name. A few scientific names are also ordinary words: Indicator indicator, the greater honeyguide, collects each paper that writes "indicator indicator". A spot check of the record sources behind 40 sampled species found that 96% of the datasets were GBIF downloads. It also found that 100% of the reference entries were Red List assessments or Birds of the World accounts.

The model needs a rated attractiveness score, and 1,472 of the 10,585 otherwise-complete birds do not have one. The same terms, without attractiveness, were fitted on both samples. The Critically Endangered term is +1.11 on the wider sample and +1.30 on the modelled one. Both values are on the log scale of the model, where the headline is +1.30. The intervals overlap: the selection changes the size a little and does not change the direction. The attention measure is the weakest input. 364 species have no English Wikipedia article and get zero, and 273 species share 129 articles, each with the full traffic of its article. With views held equal, a refit on only the species with an article approximately halves the Critically Endangered term. It changes from +0.86 to +0.43 on the same scale. Thus how much of the gap remains with attention held equal depends on the treatment of the birds without an article.

The species model stops at birds because birds are the only class with a rated-attractiveness dataset. The class figure stops at vertebrates because the Red List has assessments for few species outside them. Mammals, with a public h-index dataset of their own18, are the obvious next class, and that model is not built.

The code

The archive holds the fetch scripts, one for each source and each resumable. It also holds the build, which joins, fits and bootstraps; the figure script; and the page renderer. Each input whose license allows it is in the archive. These are AVONET and the attractiveness scores (CC BY 4.0), the Wikipedia and OpenAlex counts (CC0) and the Red List summary table (a cited aggregate). The Red List column is not in the archive. The IUCN Red List's terms (version 3.1) bar redistribution of Red List data "in their original format" (section 4). But they place "no restrictions on use of the IUCN Red List Categories associated with each named taxonomic entity" (section 3). The archive takes the cautious reading. fetch_iucn.py gets the column with a free personal token. manifest.json carries the SHA-256 of the 11,185 species and categories that this page used, 1f1d4b6f1ff2c81d. With it, build_beauty.py --verify-categories can tell a reader if their fetch matches. One shipped file, data/lc_matched.csv, lists 1,000 species that are all Least Concern, and so shows that category for them. Whether it continues to ship is an open decision.

Since February 2026, OpenAlex meters its API. The record-type counts cost $10.67 to fetch for 10,669 species. A reader who trusts the shipped counts pays nothing. A reader who fetches them again needs a key and 11 days of the allowance of a free account, or about $11 of credit.
