Three in four jobless Americans are not claiming unemployment insurance

Unemployment insurance is the part of the American safety net built for the moment a job ends. In an average week of 2025, about 26 of every 100 unemployed people claimed it. In the 1950s the figure was about 49. Where a worker lives matters: in Florida the figure was 8; in Minnesota, 46. Most of the unemployed who do not claim never apply, and many of those believe they would not qualify under their state’s rules.

  2. Job losers, the group the program is for, were about the same share of the unemployed in 2025 as in 1970, 47 against 44 in 100, while claimants decreased from 44 in 100 to 26 in 100 over the same years.
  3. In 21 states, home to 39 per cent of U.S. residents, fewer than 20 of every 100 unemployed people claimed in 2025; Florida, at 8, was lowest and Minnesota, at 46, highest.

Seventy-five years of one ratio

The U.S. Department of Labor (DOL) calls this ratio the recipiency rate1. It divides the number of people who claim regular state unemployment insurance in an average week by the number of unemployed people that the government’s household survey counts.

The 1950s: about half. From 1950 to 1959, the figure averaged 49 claimants per 100 unemployed people. The highest year was 1958, at 55.

1980 to 1984: a step down. The figure decreased from 44 in 1980 to 29 in 1984. Rebecca Blank and David Card found2 that fewer eligible unemployed people claimed between 1980 and 1982. That decrease alone took about 6 points off the figure.

1985 to 2019: lower. From 1985 to 2007, the figure stayed between 31 and 44. After the recession of 2008 and 2009 it decreased again, to 28 in 2019.

2020 to 2025: up, then down. When employers laid off millions of workers at once in 2020, the figure increased to 78. In 2025, it was 26.

Data: DOL Unemployment Insurance Chartbook, chart A.12, 1950 to 2024. This page computes 2025 from DOL’s monthly state claims reports and the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) unemployment counts. DOL’s own figure for 2025 is 28.4; it counts October 2025, a month BLS did not survey, as zero (see Method). Regular state programs only.

A falling ratio can have three causes. Fewer of the unemployed may qualify, fewer of those who qualify may apply, or states may turn down more of those who apply. The sections below give evidence on the first two.

Fifty-one programs, not one

Unemployment insurance is a federal-state system. The federal government sets broad rules and collects a payroll tax; each state and the District of Columbia decides who qualifies, how much a claimant receives each week and for how many weeks. Most states pay up to 26 weeks. A count in a Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis study3 found three states, Florida and North Carolina among them, paying at most 12 weeks in spring 2025, and eight more paying fewer than 26.

1976 to 2007. Florida’s figure was below the national one in most of these years; North Carolina’s was close to it. In 2007, the figure was 37 in Florida, 34 in North Carolina and 36 for the nation.

2008 to 2013: the cuts. In the recession, several state trust funds came close to insolvency. Seven states, Florida and North Carolina among them, then cut the maximum number of weeks. Florida’s figure had already decreased to 18 by 2011. From January 2012, Florida paid at most 23 weeks instead of 26, and 12 when its unemployment rate is below 5 per cent. North Carolina’s cut followed in July 2013.

2014 to 2025: low. In 2019, the figure was 11 in Florida and 9 in North Carolina, against 28 for the nation. In 2025, it was 8, 12 and 26.

North Carolina’s cut was, in the words of the economists Gordon Dahl and Matthew Knepper4, far larger than any earlier rollback in the United States. In July 2013, with its trust fund insolvent, the state reduced the maximum weekly benefit from $535 to $350 and the maximum weeks from 26 to 20. The maximum falls to 12 weeks when the state’s unemployment rate is below 5.5 per cent. Among the state’s short-term unemployed, the share receiving any benefit decreased from 30 per cent to 10 per cent in the three years after the change, while the share in comparable states held steady. Dahl and Knepper describe the decrease as partly mechanical, because benefits ended sooner. They say it may also be partly because people left the program earlier as payments became smaller.

Not who is unemployed, but who claims

The program is for people who lose a job through no fault of their own and who earned enough before they lost it. People who quit, or who look for work for the first time or after time away, usually do not qualify. If more of the unemployed were in those groups than in the past, the ratio would fall with no change in the program. The household survey shows little change of this kind.

1967 to 1979: close together. In 1970, job losers were 44 of every 100 unemployed people. Claimants were also 44 per 100 unemployed people.

1980 to 2025: apart. The share of job losers increases in recessions and decreases after them. In 2019, before the pandemic, job losers were 46 of every 100 unemployed people, close to the 44 of 1970. Claimants did not return to the 1970 level. They were 28 per 100 unemployed people in 2019 and 26 in 2025. In 2025, that was about 55 claimants for every 100 job losers, against about 100 in 1970.

A survey of unemployed people points the same way. In a BLS survey in 20225, 26 per cent of unemployed people who had worked in the past year had applied for benefits, and 55 per cent of those who applied received them. Of those who did not apply, 55 per cent said they believed they were not eligible. The Minneapolis Fed study estimated that 46.1 per cent of the unemployed met the main rules a survey can check, why the job ended and how long the person was out of work, in 2024. By the study’s estimate, that share decreased by 5.4 points from 2000, while the recipiency rate decreased by more than 9. Fewer maximum weeks accounted for 2.6 points of the decrease in eligibility. Changes in who was unemployed, chiefly a smaller share out of work for 26 weeks or less, accounted for 2.1.

What the research finds about the program

Three arguments favour a narrower program. The best established is that longer benefits lengthen unemployment. In Austria, where a rule gives longer benefits6 to workers with longer work histories, extending benefits from 20 to 30 weeks lowered the rate at which people found jobs in their first 20 weeks of search by 5 to 9 per cent. Whether that adds up to a large effect across a whole economy is disputed. One group of economists credited7 the end of the federal extensions in December 2013 with 2.1 million people finding work in 2014; another study, using a different method8, found that the extensions of the Great Recession raised the unemployment rate by at most 0.3 points. Dahl and Knepper also found that, within the same firm, establishments in the states that cut benefits grew employment 1.5 to 2.4 per cent faster than those in other states. Their starting salaries were 1.8 to 7.2 per cent lower.

Cost is the second argument. Unemployment insurance taxes are paid mostly by employers, on the wages they pay, and many state trust funds hold less than DOL recommends: on 1 January 2026, 18 states met DOL’s recommended solvency standard9, against 31 at the start of 2020. Paying fewer claims lets a state keep those taxes lower. The third argument is about measurement. The ratio sets an administrative count against a survey estimate, and it is not a take-up rate: its denominator includes many people the program was never designed to pay.

Research has also measured what benefits do for those who receive them. Jonathan Gruber found10 that food spending fell by an average of 7 per cent for people who became unemployed in the United States from 1968 to 1987, and that without unemployment insurance the fall would have been over three times as large. Peter Ganong and Pascal Noel found11 that spending drops sharply when benefits run out.

Method

The measure. The recipiency rate is DOL’s measure. The numerator is the number of continued weeks claimed in regular state unemployment insurance programs in a year, divided by 52. A continued week claimed is a week of unemployment for which a person who has already filed a claim asks for benefits, or for credit for the unpaid waiting week, whether or not it is paid. The denominator is the annual average number of unemployed people: the BLS Current Population Survey for the nation, and the BLS Local Area Unemployment Statistics for a state12. The numerator does not include Extended Benefits, the federal pandemic programs, or the programs for federal employees and former service members.

The years. The national figures for 1950 to 2024 and the state figures for 1976 to 2024 are DOL’s, from its Unemployment Insurance Chartbook. The build computes the same measure from the raw files: the continued weeks claimed in DOL’s monthly ETA 5159 reports13 and BLS’s monthly unemployment counts. The result agrees with DOL to within 0.05 points for every year from 2006 to 2024 for the nation, and for every state from 2020 to 2024. If a figure differs by more than 0.05 points, the build stops.

2025. BLS did not collect its household survey for October 2025, during the lapse in federal appropriations14. DOL’s published 2025 figures agree with the raw files only if October counts as zero unemployed people in a twelve-month average. Each of DOL’s 2025 figures is thus 12/11 of the figure computed with the average of the months that BLS published. This page uses the average of the eleven published months, as BLS does for its own annual average. This page’s national figure is 26.0. DOL’s figure is 28.4. The build checks every state’s DOL figure against this explanation.

Other figures. Job losers per 100 unemployed people and claimants per 100 job losers use the household survey’s count of the unemployed by reason, not seasonally adjusted, as annual averages. The share of residents uses the Census Bureau’s population estimates for July15 2025, for the 50 states and the District of Columbia. The survey results on applications, the eligibility estimates and the research results are as their authors published them.

Limits

The numerator and the denominator come from different sources. Claims are administrative counts by the state where the claim is filed; the unemployed are a survey estimate by state of residence. The ratio counts weeks claimed, not weeks paid. The survey counts a claimant who works part time and claims a partial week as employed. In 2020, for these reasons, five states and the District of Columbia recorded more claimants than unemployed people. The ratio also leaves out the federal programs, which in 2020 and 2021 paid many people that regular state programs did not, so it understates how many unemployed people received some payment in those years. DOL’s state figures before 2020 cannot be reproduced exactly from BLS’s current state counts; recomputed, the 2016 to 2019 figures differ from DOL’s by up to about 1.3 points, and the page shows DOL’s. BLS has changed the household survey’s methods over the decades, so a comparison of 1970 with 2025 is approximate. Claimants per 100 job losers is a ratio of two counts, not a share: some claimants are not job losers in the survey’s terms. Finally, the ratio describes how far the program reaches. It does not say how far it should.
