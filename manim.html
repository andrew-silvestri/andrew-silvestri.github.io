Manim Showcase

Twelve short animations from a summer spent on liquid holdup in gas gathering pipelines, drawn in Manim. They were made to explain the problem to people who had not been staring at it for three months.

Wet gas leaving a well carries liquid. Some of it drops out along the pipe and sits there, and the gas has to climb over it. The amount sitting in the line is the liquid holdup. It sets how often a line gets pigged, how big the slug catcher at the station has to be, and how much gas the line can actually move. These animations walk that chain from the map to the number.

Every network, meter cluster and site figure shown is synthetic or illustrative. The public United States outline is the only real geography, and the correlations and papers named are published work.

The problem

Why anyone cares. Liquid in the line costs throughput, costs horsepower at the station, and sizes the equipment that has to catch it.

The chart at the centre of it. After a pig run, liquid builds back toward a steady state and gas throughput sags with it. The daily figure is illustrative: 20 MMscf/d held back, priced at Henry Hub.

From the map to the pipe. Zoom into a gathering system, follow one well connect, look inside the pipe where the liquid drops out, and end at a station schematic. Two and a half minutes.

Three ways it fails

Flow regimes from stratified to slug. A slug bigger than the catcher overloads it.

Liquid carries over the catcher into compression. Compressors do not like liquid.

Liquid reaches the flare. Burning rain.

The three on one card.

Pigging

What a pig does: it sweeps the standing liquid ahead of it to the catcher. Launchers and receivers sit at the ends of the lines they serve.

Fifty years of holdup correlations, and the 1998 pig-receipt balance that ties the pig volume to the catcher size.

Getting to a number

How a holdup number is produced today: a dozen inputs into a process simulator, a station model rebuilt every time, a licence queue, slow compute. The case for a surrogate.

Where the inputs come from. Meter clusters by basin, one region zoomed, and the hand-off to a regions table of composition, temperature and pressure.

One line is one simulation run. A parallel-coordinates plot filling in as a campaign of roughly eleven thousand runs completes.

How they were made

Manim, the Python animation library, with scenes written as code and rendered to H.264. The map scenes read a small JSON of line geometry and meter points; the flow scenes are drawn from a handful of parameters. The loops here play a single cycle and are looped by the player.
